Amino Acid Calculator

One acid at a time, or a whole chain — free mass is the molecule, residue mass is what it gives to a chain.

Each disulfide subtracts 2.016 Da.

    Residue mass = free mass − 18.015 Da (average). A chain of n residues loses n−1 waters to its bonds, then gains one back for the termini: weight = sum of residues + 18.015 Da.

    The working

      How it works

      A protein’s molecular weight is the sum of its amino-acid residue masses plus one water molecule (18.015 Da average, 18.01056 Da monoisotopic) for the two termini. Residue masses are what is left after each peptide bond loses water — which is why a residue is always 18.015 Da lighter than the free amino acid.

      That is the whole rule behind this page. Pick one amino acid above: free mass is the free molecule; residue mass is what it contributes inside a chain (free − 18.015 Da).

      Checked examples

      Insulin A chain, GIVEQCCTSICSLYQLENYCN, = 2,383.71 Da average, 2,382.00 Da monoisotopic. Ubiquitin = 8,564.84 Da. Peptide GGCK = 363.43 Da by full-precision residues (363.44 Da from a two-decimal table — see the note above). Each disulfide bond subtracts 2.016 Da.

      Average mass suits lab work and SDS-PAGE; monoisotopic mass suits high-resolution mass spectrometry.

      Worked example: one acid, free against residue

      Alanine, average mass. Free 89.09 Da − 18.015 Da water = 71.075 Da, which rounds to 71.08 Da residue — exactly the table value below. The same acid in monoisotopic mass is 89.048 Da free and 71.037 Da residue (89.048 − 18.01056 = 71.037 Da, to three decimals). One acid, one water, two different mass types — never mix the average water (18.015 Da) with monoisotopic residues, or the monoisotopic water (18.01056 Da) with average residues.

      Worked example: a short chain with water and a disulfide

      Peptide CC, average mass, with its two cysteines joined by one disulfide bond. Residues first: 103.1388 + 103.1388 = 206.2776 Da. Add one water for the termini: 206.2776 + 18.015 = 224.2926 Da — that is the chain with no disulfide, 224.29 Da. Then subtract one disulfide: 224.2926 − 2.016 = 222.2766 Da, so the oxidised chain is 222.28 Da. The same sum from the printed two-decimal table (103.14 + 103.14 + 18.015 − 2.016 = 222.279 Da) also rounds to 222.28 Da, so the answer is stable either way here. Note what was not done: water was added once, not once per residue, and the disulfide was subtracted once, at the end.

      Summing free masses without subtracting water for each peptide bond is the classic beginner error — it overstates every protein by 18.015 Da per bond. Single-acid figures above are the draft’s printed two-decimal values; chain sums use full-precision residues, so a full-precision sum can differ from the printed table by 0.01 Da (cysteine free prints 121.16 Da; full-precision residues give 121.15 Da).

      Every acid is glycine plus a side chain

      All 20 standard acids share one core, C₂H₅NO₂, which sums to 2 × 12.011 + 5 × 1.008 + 14.007 + 2 × 15.999 = 75.067 Da on this site’s atomic weights. That is glycine itself: its side chain is a single hydrogen, so glycine is the core, 75.07 Da free. Every other acid is glycine plus a side-chain difference. Alanine (C₃H₇NO₂) is glycine plus CH₂, 14.027 Da: 75.067 + 14.027 = 89.094 Da, which prints as 89.09 Da free. Serine (C₃H₇NO₃) is glycine plus CH₂O, 30.026 Da: 75.067 + 30.026 = 105.093 Da, or 105.09 Da free. Cysteine (C₃H₇NO₂S) sums to 121.154 Da on these weights, which prints as 121.15 Da — the printed table’s 121.16 Da comes from a different table’s rounding, the same 0.01 Da story as GGCK. Any formula sums the same way — the Molar Mass calculator does it atom by atom.

      The water slice: small acids, big share

      The 18.015 Da of water is a fixed amount, so it is a much bigger share of a small acid than of a large one: 24.0% of glycine’s free mass, but only 8.8% of tryptophan’s. Bars are scaled to tryptophan’s free mass.

      Free, residue, or salt? Which number is on the bottle

      A bottle rarely holds the free acid alone. Hydrochloride salts add 36.46 Da per HCl: cysteine as its hydrochloride is 121.15 + 36.46 = 157.61 Da on these weights, not 121.15 Da — weighing the salt as if it were the free acid gives less amino acid than planned. Hydrates add 18.015 Da per water, the same water as everywhere else on this page. And a supplier’s certificate may quote the salt, the hydrate, or the free acid without saying which in the headline number, so read the form before comparing any single-acid weight with the table above.

      From one acid to a chain, without the 18 Da error

      The bridge from this page to the chain pages is one rule: residues, never free masses, plus one water. The AG dipeptide shows it both ways. From residues: alanine 71.0788 + glycine 57.0519 = 128.1307 Da, plus 18.015 Da of water = 146.15 Da. From free masses: 75.07 + 89.09 = 164.16 Da, minus one water for the single bond: 164.16 − 18.015 = 146.15 Da. Same answer, because the free sum double-counts exactly one water per bond. Longer chains follow the same rule — the Peptide calculator adds disulfides and capped termini, and the Protein Molecular Weight calculator applies it to full proteins.

      Traps worth knowing

      1. Free or residue? The beginner error: summing free masses overstates a chain by 18.015 Da per bond. Residues first, always.
      2. Water counted twice? One water per chain, not per residue and not per terminus. n residues, n−1 waters lost, one water back.
      3. Disulfides. Each bond subtracts 2.016 Da. A chain with cysteines does not prove a bond exists — count only the bonds you know are there.
      4. Ile against Leu. Identical mass (isomers). No mass calculator can tell them apart; the sequence must.
      5. Salt form. A TFA or HCl salt weighs more than the free peptide. Check whether the supplier’s weight includes the counter-ion.
      6. Average or monoisotopic? Picking the wrong one explains most small mismatches with a supplier’s certificate, before any other cause.
      7. Modified termini. An N-acetyl terminus adds 42.011 Da and a C-amide terminus subtracts 0.984 Da — apply both corrections, or a capped peptide will look wrong by exactly those amounts.
      8. Unknown letters silently skipped. Only the 20 standard one-letter codes count, so a sequence containing B, Z, X, U or O has those positions skipped from the total (the calculator flags them) — check the residue count against the sequence length.
      9. Mixed mass types. Never pair the average water (18.015 Da) with monoisotopic residues, or the monoisotopic water (18.01056 Da) with average residues. Each bond then slips by 0.004 Da — invisible for one acid, visible across a long chain.
      10. Hydrochloride weighed as free acid. A salt is heavier than its acid by 36.46 Da per HCl. Weighing the salt as if it were the free acid gives less amino acid than planned, and the shortfall grows with every HCl in the form.
      The 18 Da error is the famous one. A result about 18 Da — or a multiple of it — away from the expected value is almost always a water-count error: free masses summed, termini added twice, or one water per residue. Count bonds: n residues, n−1 waters lost, one water back.

      Questions people actually ask

      What is the difference between free mass and residue mass?

      Free mass is the free amino-acid molecule. Residue mass is what it contributes inside a chain, after losing water when its peptide bond forms — always 18.015 Da less (average). Alanine: 89.09 Da free, 71.08 Da residue.

      Why is water subtracted at all?

      Forming a peptide bond releases H2O. A chain of n residues has n−1 bonds, so it loses (n−1) × 18.015 Da — then one water is added back for the two free termini, which is why the formula is sum of residues + 18.015.

      Average or monoisotopic — which should I use?

      Average for lab work and SDS-PAGE; monoisotopic for high-resolution mass spectrometry. The difference is small for one acid (alanine: 89.09 vs 89.048 Da) and grows with chain length.

      Why do results vary slightly between sources?

      Different sources use different precision or older atomic weight values. This site uses one canonical value per compound everywhere. Check, in order: average vs monoisotopic, cysteine state, termini, salt vs free base, and non-standard residues.

      Which amino acid is heaviest, and which is lightest?

      Tryptophan is heaviest (204.23 Da free, 186.21 Da residue); glycine is lightest (75.07 Da free, 57.05 Da residue).

      How do I calculate a peptide’s mass from this table?

      Add the residue masses, then add 18.015 Da (average) for the termini, then apply corrections: −2.016 Da per disulfide, +42.011 Da for N-acetyl, −0.984 Da for C-amide. Worked: GGCK = 363.43 Da average by full-precision residues.

      Why do isoleucine and leucine have the same mass?

      They are isomers with the same formula, C6H13NO2, arranged differently, so both are 131.17 Da free and 113.16 Da residue. No mass calculation can tell them apart; only the sequence can.

      What does a disulfide bond do to a peptide’s mass?

      Each disulfide bond subtracts 2.016 Da, because two hydrogens are lost when the bond forms. Worked: CC is 224.29 Da with no disulfide and 222.28 Da with one. Count only bonds you know exist — two cysteines in a sequence do not prove a bond.

      Does this calculator include the salt form my supplier sells?

      No — it gives the free peptide. A TFA or HCl salt weighs more, because the counter-ion is included in the supplier’s weight but not in the free-peptide mass, so check the certificate of analysis for salt against free base before comparing numbers.

      What if my protein has modified residues, not just the standard 20?

      Then the 20-acid table alone is not enough: each modification changes the mass (for example phosphorylation and glycosylation add mass the standard residue does not carry). Work out the modified residue’s mass first, then use the same rule — residues plus one water, then disulfide and termini corrections.

      How do I work out a free mass from the formula?

      Add the atomic weights of every atom. Glycine, C₂H₅NO₂, is 2 × 12.011 + 5 × 1.008 + 14.007 + 2 × 15.999 = 75.07 Da. The Molar Mass calculator does any formula the same way.

      Why does cysteine print as 121.16 in the table but 121.15 from full precision?

      Different tables round differently. The printed two-decimal table says 121.16 Da; summing full-precision atoms on this site’s weights gives 121.154 Da, which prints as 121.15 Da. Same molecule, 0.01 Da of table rounding — the GGCK story in miniature.

      What does a hydrochloride salt add?

      36.46 Da per HCl. Cysteine hydrochloride is 121.15 + 36.46 = 157.61 Da on these weights, against 121.15 Da for the free acid.

      How do I go from one acid to a whole chain?

      Add residue masses, never free masses, then add one water (18.015 Da average) for the termini, then disulfide and termini corrections. The Peptide calculator does short chains with corrections; the Protein Molecular Weight calculator does full proteins.
