What a comparison should actually tell you

A sorted column of numbers is half the job. For each compound you want the rank with ties marked, the gap to the next heavier one, the ratio to the lightest, and the moles a fixed mass gives — because that last one is what you weigh.

Glucose and fructose tie, and so do sucrose and lactose, because each pair shares a formula. Molecular weight cannot separate isomers, and a batch that quietly dropped the second of each pair would be hiding that.

The two gaps happen to be identical: 180.156 − 18.015 and 342.297 − 180.156 both come to 162.141 g/mol, which is C6H10O5 — a sugar unit less a water, twice over.

Heavier means fewer moles

10 g of glucose is 0.0555 mol; 10 g of sucrose is 0.0292 mol, about half. For a fixed mass, moles move the opposite way to molar mass — which is the whole reason to compare in moles rather than grams.

The sugars ladder, as bars

Reading the gaps: one residue, twice

The two gaps in the sugars batch are identical: 180.156 − 18.015 = 162.141, and 342.297 − 180.156 = 162.141. That number is C6H10O5 — a hexose sugar less one water — and it is the condensation step itself: glucose + fructose − water = sucrose, 180.156 × 2 − 18.015 = 342.297 exactly. The ladder reads water → add one sugar residue → glucose → add another → sucrose. Gaps in a batch are chemistry, not just arithmetic.

Hydration reshuffles the ranking

The hydrate-pairs batch proves that a ranking depends on the forms, not just the families. Sorted lightest first: Na2CO3 105.988, MgSO4 120.361, CuSO4 159.602, MgSO4·7H2O 246.466, CuSO4·5H2O 249.677, Na2CO3·10H2O 286.138 g/mol. The gaps run 14.373, 39.241, 86.864, 3.211, 36.461. Notice the middle: the two heptahydrate/pentahydrate forms sit 3.211 g/mol apart, while their anhydrous parents sit 39.241 apart — hydration pulled magnesium sulfate past copper sulfate and parked the two hydrates side by side. The waters are 51.17% of MgSO4·7H2O and 62.96% of Na2CO3·10H2O. Compare forms, or the ranking misleads.

What a weight ranking cannot tell you

Glucose and fructose tie on weight and differ everywhere else — structure, sweetness, metabolism. A weight ranking answers only the weight question. For what else differs, the Percent Composition calculator breaks each formula into mass shares, and properties beyond composition (solubility, toxicity, behaviour) belong to reference data, not to a molar mass. Never read a tie as an identity, and never read a rank as a quality score.

Moles per fixed mass, across the sugars

For solution work the ranking inverts: the lightest compound gives the most moles per gram. Per 100 g: water 5.551 mol, glucose 0.555 mol, sucrose 0.292 mol. Ten grams told the same story at a tenth of the scale (0.5551, 0.0555, 0.0292 mol) — the fixed mass is arbitrary, the inversion is not. The Grams to Moles calculator runs this conversion for any single compound.

Try it yourself

  1. The sugars batch gaps are both 162.141 g/mol. What formula is that, and what reaction does it describe?
  2. Sort Na2CO3, MgSO4, CuSO4, MgSO4·7H2O, CuSO4·5H2O, Na2CO3·10H2O lightest first. Which two hydrates land side by side?
  3. How many moles are in 100 g of glucose and 100 g of sucrose?

Answers: (1) C6H10O5, a hexose less water — the condensation step; glucose + fructose − water = sucrose. (2) Na2CO3 105.988, MgSO4 120.361, CuSO4 159.602, MgSO4·7H2O 246.466, CuSO4·5H2O 249.677, Na2CO3·10H2O 286.138 — the two sulfate hydrates, 3.211 apart. (3) 100 ÷ 180.156 = 0.555 mol; 100 ÷ 342.297 = 0.292 mol.

Before you paste

  1. One compound per line.
  2. Check capitals. Co is cobalt, CO is carbon monoxide. Both parse, so neither is flagged — only you can tell which you meant.
  3. Check hydrates and salt forms. CuSO4 understates CuSO4·5H2O by about 36%.
  4. Ties are isomers, not errors.
  5. Sort by weight, not alphabetically. C12… sorts before C6… alphabetically and is heavier.
  6. Round for display only. Two weights within 0.1 g/mol can swap order after early rounding.
  7. A bad line should be flagged, not fatal.
  8. Accepting the first form of an ambiguous name. Copper sulfate and washing soda each cover several forms, and the page calculates the first while flagging the rest. The flag is the point — check it before trusting the row.
  9. Comparing weights across different bases. Ranking an anhydrous salt against a hydrate as if they were one family misleads: hydration alone moves MgSO4 from 120.361 to 246.466 g/mol. State the form on every row.

Questions people actually ask

Why do two rows show the same weight?

They are isomers or duplicates. Glucose and fructose are both C6H12O6 = 180.156 g/mol. One formula always gives one weight, but one weight does not guarantee one compound.

What happens to a mistyped line?

It is listed by line number with the reason, and every other line still calculates.

Does it sort by formula or by weight?

By weight, on unrounded values.

Can I paste names instead of formulas?

Yes. Names are resolved first, and a name covering several forms — copper sulfate, washing soda — is flagged so you can pick the right one.

How many lines can I paste?

Up to 25 at a time. Split a longer list into chunks.

Why do the gaps repeat in the sugars batch?

Because each step adds the same residue: C6H10O5 (162.141 g/mol), a hexose less water. Water + residue = glucose; glucose + residue = sucrose. Identical gaps are chemistry, not coincidence.

Can I compare hydrates with anhydrous salts?

Yes, but state every form — hydration reshuffles the ranking. MgSO4·7H2O (246.466) sits next to CuSO4·5H2O (249.677), while anhydrous MgSO4 (120.361) sits far below anhydrous CuSO4 (159.602).

What does the ratio column mean?

Each weight divided by the lightest weight in the batch. Glucose at 10.00× weighs ten times the lightest compound (water, 18.015 g/mol); sucrose at 19.00× weighs nineteen times it.

How do I turn the ranking into moles?

Divide the fixed mass by each molar mass: 100 g of glucose is 100 ÷ 180.156 = 0.555 mol; 100 g of sucrose is 0.292 mol. The Grams to Moles calculator does this for any single compound.
