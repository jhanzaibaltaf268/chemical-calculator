Dilution Calculator

How much stock to take, how much diluent to add — and when a single step asks for a volume too small to pipette, the serial plan that replaces it.

One unit for both, so they can never be mixed.

The true stock is the label times this.

Try:
—

    C₁V₁ = C₂V₂ works because dilution adds solvent but no solute, so the amount C × V does not change.

    The working

      Diluent is what you add; V₂ is what you end up with. Confusing the two is the most common dilution error.

      What goes in the flask

      Drawn to scale. When the stock slice is almost invisible, the volume is probably too small to pipette accurately.

      The plan

      Diluent means “top up to”, not “add on top”

      Adding 99.5 mL of diluent to 0.5 mL of stock gives 100 mL. Adding 100 mL to 0.5 mL gives 100.5 mL, and the concentration is wrong. With a bigger stock volume the error grows: 20 mL of stock plus 200 mL of diluent is 220 mL, so the result lands at 200/220 = 0.909 of what you wanted. Make the final volume in a volumetric flask and top up to the mark.

      Worked by hand: 100 mL of 1 µM from a 10 mM stock

      10 mM is 10,000 µM, so the factor is 10,000×. In one step, V₁ = (1 × 100) ÷ 10,000 = 0.01 mL, which is 10 µL. The arithmetic is right and the pipetting is not: a small error at 10 µL becomes a large error in the result.

      Split it into two 100× steps instead. Make 10 mL of 100 µM by taking 100 µL of stock into 9.9 mL of diluent; then make 100 mL of 1 µM by taking 1.0 mL of that into 99.0 mL. The cumulative factor is 100 × 100 = 10,000, and every volume is comfortable.

      Not every large number needs splitting. The same 10 mM stock to 50 µM in 100 mL needs 0.5 mL — 500 µL, perfectly pipettable — so one step is right there.

      Reading dilution notation

      WrittenStock : diluentPartsFactorStrength
      1:21 : 122×1/2
      1:51 : 455×1/5
      1:101 : 91010×1/10
      1:1001 : 99100100×1/100
      10× stock to 1×1 : 91010×1/10
      Some protocols write 1:10 to mean one part stock plus ten parts diluent — eleven parts total, so 1/11. That is a 10% difference in the final concentration. If a protocol does not say “total”, confirm before pipetting.

      Purity-corrected stocks

      A stock made from powder is really its label times the assay purity. A stock labelled 10 mg/mL at 95% purity is 9.5 mg/mL. To make 50 mL of 1 mg/mL you need (1 × 50) ÷ 9.5 = 5.26 mL, not 5.0 mL — using the uncorrected figure lands you at 0.95 mg/mL, 5% low. For hydrated or salt forms, take the molar mass from the vial label rather than from memory.

      Solve for any of the four

      The equation rearranges three more ways, and each answers a real question. Final concentration: 2 mL of 5 mg/mL made up to 10 mL gives C₂ = 5 × 2 ÷ 10 = 1 mg/mL, a 5-fold dilution. Final volume: 10 mL of 10 mM diluted to 1 mM gives V₂ = 10 × 10 ÷ 1 = 100 mL. Stock concentration: 20 mL made up to 200 mL at 1× came from C₁ = 1 × 200 ÷ 20 = 10× — the buffer-stock question, answered backwards. Any three known values determine the fourth.

      One step against two steps, as slices

      The 10,000× example again, as shares of the flask. A single step takes 0.01 mL of stock in 100 mL — 0.01% of the flask, effectively invisible. The split plan takes 100 µL in 10 mL (1%) and then 1.0 mL in 100 mL (1%) — two comfortable slices instead of one impossible one.

      Step 1 of 2 — 100 µL in 10 mL (1%)

      The single-step bar is drawn at a minimum visible width; its true share is 0.01%.

      The 1:10 ratio, worked both ways

      The table above reads 1:10 as one part stock plus nine parts diluent: ten parts total, DF 10, strength 1/10. The other convention reads it as one part stock plus ten parts diluent: eleven parts total, DF 11, strength 1/11. For 1,000 mL under the second convention that is 1,000 ÷ 11 = 90.91 mL of concentrate and 909.09 mL of water, at 9.09% strength — 9.09% below the intended 10%. The notation is identical; the result is not. Confirm the convention before pipetting.

      Concentrating is not a dilution

      Wanting 50 mL of 2 M from a 1 M stock gives V₁ = 2 × 50 ÷ 1 = 100 mL — more stock than the final volume, which is impossible by dilution. The calculator refuses this case on purpose: C₂ above C₁ is not a calculation, it is a different task — evaporate solvent, or start from a stronger stock. A V₁ larger than V₂ is the same signal in any rearrangement.

      Where the stock concentration comes from

      Dilution starts from a stock whose concentration is already known — and when it is made from powder, that concentration comes from mass, molar mass and volume, which is the Molarity calculator’s job. At the other end, the diluted concentration is what downstream work consumes: a protein solution diluted to its working strength is then read as mg/mL to µM by the Protein Molarity calculator. Dilution sits between the two; neither end is guessed.

      Try it yourself

      1. 2 mL of 5 mg/mL made up to 10 mL: what is the final concentration, and the dilution factor?
      2. 1,000 mL at 1:10 under the “one part plus ten parts” convention: how much concentrate, how much water, what strength?
      3. 50 mL of 2 M from a 1 M stock: what does the equation give, and why is it refused?

      Answers: (1) 5 × 2 ÷ 10 = 1 mg/mL; DF 5. (2) 90.91 mL concentrate + 909.09 mL water; 9.09% strength, 9.09% below 10%. (3) V₁ = 100 mL, more than the 50 mL final volume — concentrating, not diluting, so a stronger stock or evaporation is needed.

      Sources: the dilution equation is mole conservation, C × V unchanged by adding solvent; the page’s own arithmetic throughout.

      Traps worth knowing

      • Diluent against final volume. Add V₂ − V₁, never V₂.
      • Swapped C₁ and C₂. If V₁ comes out larger than V₂, the inputs are the wrong way round.
      • Thousand-fold unit slips. mM against µM, mL against µL, mg against µg. This is the usual cause of a 1,000× error.
      • Nominal against true stock concentration. Correct for purity before the equation, not after.
      • Assuming volumes simply add. Fine for dilute aqueous solutions; not for concentrated mixtures — 50 mL of ethanol plus 50 mL of water is about 96 mL. Top up to the mark.
      • Rounding volumes early. 5.26 mL rounded to 5.3 shifts the result by 0.8%. At the microlitre scale, rounding to the nearest µL can shift it by 10%.
      • Compounding error down a series. Every transfer carries its own error. Use the largest practical transfer volume, change tips each step, and mix every tube fully.
      • 1:10 without a convention. One part plus nine (DF 10) here, one part plus ten (DF 11) elsewhere — a 9.09% difference in the final concentration. The table on this page states its convention; a protocol that does not must be confirmed.
      • Wrong molar mass for the form. A stock made from a hydrate or a salt uses the vial label’s form, not the anhydrous formula. The wrong molar mass makes the stock concentration wrong before dilution even starts.

      Questions people actually ask

      How much diluent do I add?

      V₂ − V₁. For 0.5 mL of stock made up to 100 mL, that is 99.5 mL.

      When should I use a serial dilution?

      When one step would need a volume too small to pipette accurately, or the factor is above about 1,000×. For 10,000×, two 100× steps.

      Do C₁ and C₂ need the same units?

      Yes — the same kind and the same scale. This calculator uses one unit selector for both so they cannot drift apart. Volumes must match each other, but need not match the concentrations.

      Does purity change the calculation?

      Yes. Correct C₁ first. A 95%-pure stock labelled 10 mg/mL is really 9.5 mg/mL, so 50 mL of 1 mg/mL needs 5.26 mL.

      Do 1:10, 10× and a factor of 10 mean the same thing?

      Here, yes: one part stock plus nine parts diluent, ten parts total, one tenth the strength. Some protocols use 1:10 differently, so confirm the convention.

      Can I solve for C₁, C₂ or V₂ instead of V₁?

      Yes — the equation rearranges. C₂ = C₁V₁ ÷ V₂ (2 mL of 5 mg/mL to 10 mL gives 1 mg/mL); V₂ = C₁V₁ ÷ C₂ (10 mL of 10 mM to 1 mM gives 100 mL); C₁ = C₂V₂ ÷ V₁ (20 mL to 200 mL at 1× came from a 10× stock).

      What if my target is stronger than my stock?

      That is concentrating, not diluting, and no dilution step achieves it: 50 mL of 2 M from a 1 M stock would need 100 mL of stock, more than the final volume. Evaporate solvent or start from a stronger stock.

      Why is 1:10 sometimes 11 parts?

      Two conventions share the notation. This page reads 1:10 as one part stock plus nine parts diluent (10 total, DF 10). Some protocols read it as one part plus ten parts diluent (11 total, DF 11): 1,000 mL then needs 90.91 mL of concentrate and 909.09 mL of water, at 9.09% strength.

      How do I know the stock concentration to enter?

      From the label, corrected for purity — or, for a stock made from powder, from mass, molar mass and volume, which is the Molarity calculator’s job. Never enter a guessed stock concentration.
