DNA Calculator

DNA weight two ways — from the bases you have, or from the length you know.

A sequence gives GC content too; a length alone gives the weight only.

Try:
—

    Length estimate, canonical basis stated: 660 Da per base pair double-stranded, 330 Da per base single-stranded. A base-exact weight needs the sequence and the end chemistry.

    The working

      Length times the per-unit rule. The strand choice doubles or halves the answer, so it comes first.

      Weight at a glance

      One rule per strand

      double-stranded weight = base pairs × 660 Da and single-stranded weight = bases × 330 Da. The 660 figure is two 330-Da strands averaged over base composition; it is an estimate, not a count of the actual bases.

      Worked by hand: 670 base pairs, double-stranded

      1. Length. 670 bp.
      2. Multiply. 670 × 660 = 442,200 Da = 442.2 kDa.

      With a sequence instead, the same rule applies to its length, and GC content follows: ACGTACGTAC is 10 bases, 5 of them G or C, so 50.0% GC; single-stranded it weighs 10 × 330 = 3,300 Da.

      Worked by hand: 20-base single-stranded primer

      1. Length and GC content. ATGCGTACGTAGCTAGCTAA is 20 bases, 9 of them G or C, so 9 ÷ 20 × 100 = 45.0% GC.
      2. Multiply. 20 × 330 = 6,600 Da = 6.6 kDa (estimate).

      The same 20 units double-stranded would be 20 × 660 = 13,200 Da — the strand factor again, not a different length.

      Worked by hand: 5.2 kilobases, double-stranded

      1. Convert. 5.2 kb = 5.2 × 1,000 = 5,200 bp.
      2. Multiply. 5,200 × 660 = 3,432,000 Da = 3,432 kDa = 3.432 MDa (estimate).

      Exact when you have the sequence: sum the residues

      The length rule is an estimate. The exact weight sums the actual residues: A 313.2, T 304.2, C 289.2, G 329.2 Da. Worked for ACGTACGTAC, single-stranded: A 3 × 313.2 + C 3 × 289.2 + G 2 × 329.2 + T 2 × 304.2 = 939.6 + 867.6 + 658.4 + 608.4 = 3,074.0 Da. The length rule says 10 × 330 = 3,300 Da — higher, because it averages over compositions instead of counting this sequence’s bases. Then the end chemistry: a synthesised strand with 5′ and 3′ hydroxyl ends subtracts about 62 Da (some tools use 61 Da — the same correction, rounded differently), giving 3,074.0 − 62 = 3,012.0 Da for the synthesised form. RNA sums the same way with its own residue weights (A 329.2, U 306.2, C 305.2, G 345.2) — the RNA calculator states its 5′ end.

      Double-stranded, exactly: sum both strands

      Doubling the sense strand is wrong for an exact weight, because the complementary strand has different residues. ACGTACGTAC pairs with TGCATGCATG: T 3 × 304.2 + G 3 × 329.2 + C 2 × 289.2 + A 2 × 313.2 = 912.6 + 987.6 + 578.4 + 626.4 = 3,105.0 Da — 31 Da more than the sense strand, because the antisense carries more of the heavy G. The exact double-stranded weight, synthesised form, is 3,074.0 + 3,105.0 − 2 × 62 = 6,055.0 Da, against 6,600 Da by the length rule. A restriction fragment adds 79 Da per 5′ monophosphate the enzyme leaves: 6,055.0 + 2 × 79 = 6,213.0 Da.

      A 313.2 ↔ T 304.2C 289.2 ↔ G 329.2G 329.2 ↔ C 289.2T 304.2 ↔ A 313.2

      Each pair pops in: a base and its complement weigh differently, so both strands must be summed.

      Why two tables differ by 18 Da per base

      Some tables list A 331.2, T 322.2, G 347.2, C 307.2 Da — exactly 18.0 Da more per base than the residue set above. Neither table is wrong: the larger set lists free nucleotides, the smaller lists in-chain residues, and each phosphodiester bond loses one water (18.015 Da) when it forms. It is the same water story as proteins. The rule is simple: sum residues for a chain, never free nucleotides — and use one table consistently, with that table’s terminal correction.

      Which form is your DNA?

      Pick the correction by the ends, not by the name. A synthetic oligo with 5′ and 3′ hydroxyl ends subtracts about 62 Da per strand. A restriction fragment adds 79 Da per 5′ monophosphate the enzyme leaves. A circular plasmid has no free ends, so it takes neither correction. Ends unknown or unstated: use the length rule on this page, and state its basis (660 Da/bp double-stranded, 330 Da/base single-stranded) when you quote the number.

      Single against double, by the length rule

      The same 10 units, both strands by the page’s rule:

      Single-stranded, 10 bases — 3,300 Da

      Double-stranded, 10 bp — 6,600 Da

      The strand factor again: double the strands, double the weight.

      From weight to moles

      The weight exists so a mass can become moles. Picomoles = nanograms × 1,000 ÷ weight in Da. Worked: 250 ng of the 670 bp fragment (442,200 Da) is 250 × 1,000 ÷ 442,200 = 0.565 pmol. And a concentration: 10 µL of a 50 ng/µL solution of the same fragment holds 500 ng, which is 500 × 1,000 ÷ 442,200 = 1.131 pmol in 10 µL, or 113.07 nM.       Try it yourself

      1. ACGTACGTAC single-stranded, residue sum: A 3 × 313.2 + C 3 × 289.2 + G 2 × 329.2 + T 2 × 304.2. What is it, and what does the synthesised form weigh?
      2. The same sequence double-stranded, synthesised form: sense 3,074.0 Da, antisense 3,105.0 Da. What is the total?
      3. 660 ng of a 1,000 bp double-stranded fragment (660,000 Da). How many picomoles?

      Answers: (1) 3,074.0 Da; synthesised, 3,074.0 − 62 = 3,012.0 Da. (2) 3,074.0 + 3,105.0 − 124 = 6,055.0 Da. (3) 660 × 1,000 ÷ 660,000 = 1 pmol.

      Sources: ThermoFisher residue weights; SelectScience nucleic-acid sheet (terminal corrections).

      Traps worth knowing

      1. Base pairs or bases? A 670 bp double-stranded fragment is 1,340 bases of sequence. Multiplying bases by 660 doubles the answer.
      2. Single or double? The strand choice is a factor of two. Primers and oligos are single-stranded; plasmids and fragments are usually double-stranded.
      3. Estimate, not exact. 660 Da/bp averages base composition. A GC-rich fragment weighs slightly more per pair than an AT-rich one; for an exact weight, sum the actual residues.
      4. Ends unstated. Phosphorylated, hydroxyl or overhanging ends shift the weight. The length rule assumes a standard linear fragment.
      5. Kilobases. 1 kb is 1,000 bp, not 1,024. A 4.42 kb plasmid is about 2,917 kDa double-stranded by this rule.
      6. Salt form unstated. 617.96 Da/bp is the sodium-salt figure, not the 660 Da/bp free-acid estimate used here. State the basis before comparing two weights.
      7. Letters outside A, T, G and C. Anything else in the sequence — U, N, spaces — is not counted as a base. Clean the sequence first instead of assuming the entered length was used.
      8. Doubling an exact strand weight. Doubling works for the length estimate, but not for an exact sum: the complementary strand has different residues. Sum both strands separately for an exact double-stranded weight.
      9. Summing free nucleotides into a chain. The free-nucleotide table runs 18 Da per base above the residue table. Summing it as if it were residues overstates a 10-base chain by 180 Da — use residues for chains, always.
      10. Forgetting the terminal correction on an exact sum. A residue sum without its end correction describes a different form: subtract about 62 Da per synthesised strand, or add 79 Da per restriction phosphate, or the exact number answers a question nobody asked.
      The strand factor is the expensive one. Everything else here is a single multiplication, so entering bases where base pairs belong — or the reverse — is a clean factor-of-two error that no rounding fixes. State the strand before the length.

      Questions people actually ask

      How much does DNA weigh per base pair?

      About 660 Da per base pair double-stranded, 330 Da per base single-stranded. 670 bp double-stranded is 442,200 Da.

      Is 660 Da/bp exact?

      No — it is an average over base composition. It is the standard quick estimate; a base-exact weight needs the sequence summed residue by residue.

      How do I calculate GC content?

      Count G plus C, divide by the total bases, times 100. ACGTACGTAC is 5 of 10, so 50.0% GC. A length alone cannot give GC content.

      Single-stranded or double-stranded for a primer?

      Single-stranded: use 330 Da per base. A 20-base primer is about 6,600 Da by this rule.

      Why does another source give a different weight for the same fragment?

      Usually the per-unit rule (660 vs 617.96 Da/bp for the sodium salt form, or a base-exact sum) or the end chemistry. Check which basis the other source states before assuming either is wrong.

      How much does 1 kb of double-stranded DNA weigh?

      About 660,000 Da, or 660 kDa, by this rule: 1,000 × 660 = 660,000 Da.

      How do I convert daltons to kilodaltons or megadaltons?

      Divide by 1,000 for kDa and by 1,000,000 for MDa. So 3,432,000 Da = 3,432 kDa = 3.432 MDa.

      How do I turn nanograms into picomoles?

      Picomoles = nanograms × 1,000 ÷ molecular weight in Da. For example, 660 ng of a 1,000 bp double-stranded fragment (660,000 Da) is 660 × 1,000 ÷ 660,000 = 1 pmol.

      Does a circular plasmid weigh the same as a linear fragment of the same length?

      By this length rule, yes — the estimate is the same. Exactly, the end chemistry differs: a circular molecule has no free ends, so a residue-by-residue sum must use the circular form, not linear end assumptions.

      How do I calculate double-stranded DNA weight exactly?

      Sum both strands separately, because the complement has different residues. ACGTACGTAC: sense 3,074.0 Da, antisense 3,105.0 Da, synthesised total 3,074.0 + 3,105.0 − 124 = 6,055.0 Da. Doubling the sense strand misses the 31 Da difference.

      Why do two tables differ by 18 Da per base?

      One lists free nucleotides, the other in-chain residues, and each phosphodiester bond loses one water (18.015 Da) when it forms. Sum residues for a chain, never free nucleotides.

      What does a restriction cut add?

      79 Da per 5′ monophosphate the enzyme leaves — 79 Da for a single strand, 158 Da for a double-stranded fragment. A synthesised strand with hydroxyl ends subtracts about 62 Da per strand instead.

      What is GC content good for?

      GC pairs carry three hydrogen bonds against AT’s two, so GC-rich DNA melts at a higher temperature. GC content feeds melting-temperature estimates and primer design — and it is why the length rule is only an average: a GC-rich fragment weighs slightly more per pair than an AT-rich one.

      Can I use this for RNA?

      Not with DNA weights: RNA uses U instead of T and different residue weights (A 329.2, U 306.2, C 305.2, G 345.2 Da), and a transcript carries a 5′ triphosphate. Use the RNA calculator, which states its 5′ end.
