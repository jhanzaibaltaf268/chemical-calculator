Element Mass Calculator

How much of each element is in the sample you weighed — and, in reverse, how much compound to weigh for a target amount of one element.

    This is percent composition multiplied by your sample mass. It is not the mass of a pure element from a mole count.

    The working

      The mass fraction is the part that does the work; the sample mass just scales it.

      Grams of each element

      Total

      Element masses must add back to the sample mass. That sum is the built-in check.

      Atoms in one formula unit

      One circle per atom, sized by atomic weight and coloured by the CPK convention used in molecular models. Hydrogen is often most of the atoms and very little of the mass.

      The method

      mass of element = sample mass × (atoms × atomic weight) ÷ molar mass. Reverse it by dividing instead: compound mass = target element mass ÷ mass fraction.

      Worked by hand: 250 g of CaCO3

      Atoms: Ca 1, C 1, O 3. Masses per mole: 40.078, 12.011, and 3 × 15.999 = 47.997. Molar mass 100.086 g/mol, so the fractions are Ca 40.04%, C 12.00%, O 47.96% — summing to exactly 100.00%.

      In 250 g: calcium 100.10 g, carbon 30.00 g, oxygen 119.90 g. Those add to 250.00 g, which is the check.

      Supplement labels. A 500 mg calcium carbonate tablet holds 500 × 0.40042 = about 200 mg of elemental calcium. The 500 mg is the weight of the salt. To get 200 mg of calcium you need 499.5 mg of the carbonate — which is why the two numbers on a label differ. The same trap applies to citrate and gluconate salts.

      Hydrates carry their water into every row

      CuSO4·5H2O is 249.677 g/mol. In 100 g there are 36.08 g of water, and the element rows count the water’s atoms too: Cu 25.45 g, S 12.84 g, H 4.04 g, O 57.67 g. Heating drives off the 36.08 g and leaves about 63.92 g of anhydrous salt.

      The reverse direction, worked

      Want 10 g of copper from copper(II) oxide? CuO is 63.546 ÷ 79.545 = 79.89% copper, so the compound to weigh is 10 ÷ 0.7989 = 12.52 g of CuO. And that 12.52 g also brings 12.52 × 20.11% = 2.52 g of oxygen — the reverse mode’s “also brings” rows, which matter whenever the other elements are contaminants, reactants, or part of the next step. The fractions are the Percent Composition calculator’s output; the sample mass just scales them, in either direction.

      Elemental against oxide labelling

      Fertilizer labels report oxides, not elements, and the gap is large. P2O5 is 2 × 30.974 + 5 × 15.999 = 141.943 g/mol, of which phosphorus is 61.948 ÷ 141.943 = 43.64%. So a fertilizer labelled 20% P2O5 carries only 20 × 43.64% = 8.73% elemental phosphorus — and supplying 10 g of elemental P from it needs 10 ÷ 0.0873 = 114.6 g of fertilizer. Potash is the same story: K2O is 94.195 g/mol at 83.02% potassium, so a 60% K2O potash is 49.81% elemental K. Bars are the P2O5 label against the elemental reality, scaled to the label.

      Label: 20% P₂O₅

      Elemental P inside: 8.73%

      The label number is not the element number. Convert before comparing fertilizers.

      The check that catches broken arithmetic

      Take 652.4 g of MgO: MgO is 24.305 + 15.999 = 40.304 g/mol, so the sample holds 652.4 ÷ 40.304 = 16.187 mol, giving 16.187 × 24.305 = 393.4 g of Mg and 16.187 × 15.999 = 259.0 g of O — and 393.4 + 259.0 = 652.4, the sample mass, exactly. If a worked answer’s parts total far more than the sample — hundreds of grams more, not hundredths from rounding — the arithmetic is wrong, not the method. Add the parts back, every time.

      From a solution to an element

      A dissolved compound needs one step first: concentration × volume = compound mass, then the element mass as usual. Worked: 250 mL of 2.00 M CaCl2 holds 0.250 × 2.00 = 0.500 mol, which is 0.500 × 110.978 = 55.489 g of CaCl2; calcium is 40.078 ÷ 110.978 = 36.11% of that, so 20.04 g of calcium. The Molarity calculator handles the concentration-and-volume step.

      Purity, worked once in each direction

      Purity scales once, in both directions. Forward: 250 g of CaCO3 at 90% purity holds 250 × 40.04% × 0.90 = 90.09 g of calcium. Reverse: 100 g of calcium from the same 90%-pure carbonate needs 100 ÷ (0.4004 × 0.90) = 277.5 g — not 249.7 g, which is the pure-compound figure. Forgetting the purity factor in reverse is the same error as applying it twice forward.

      3. 250 mL of 2.00 M CaCl2: how many grams of calcium does it hold?

      Traps worth knowing

      • Confusing atom count with mass share. CaCO3 is one fifth calcium by atom count and 40.04% by mass.
      • Forgetting bracket multipliers. (NH4)2 holds 2 N and 8 H.
      • Dropping hydrate water. Using CuSO4 for CuSO4·5H2O understates the molar mass by 36% and changes every row.
      • Reading a supplement label as salt mass. See the note above.
      • Applying purity twice. Scale once, and follow the certificate’s own definition.
      • Mixing mg and g. Convert before multiplying, not after.
      • An element that is not in the formula. Its mass is 0 g. That is an answer, not an error.
      • Forgetting the “also brings” rows. Weighing 12.52 g of CuO for 10 g of copper also brings 2.52 g of oxygen. If the other elements feed the next step or count as contamination, they need accounting, not ignoring.
      • Forgetting purity in reverse. 100 g of calcium needs 249.7 g of pure CaCO3 but 277.5 g of 90%-pure carbonate. Divide by the fraction times the purity, not by the fraction alone.

      Questions people actually ask

      How much calcium is in a 500 mg calcium carbonate tablet?

      About 200 mg of elemental calcium. Supplement labels usually state the elemental amount, not the weight of the salt.

      I need 100 g of calcium — how much CaCO3 do I weigh?

      Divide by the mass fraction: 100 ÷ 0.40042 = 249.7 g. Switch the calculator to reverse mode and it does this for any element.

      How does a hydrate change the answer?

      The water’s atoms count. In CuSO4·5H2O water is 36.08% of the mass, and the hydrogen and oxygen rows include it.

      What if my sample is not pure?

      Set the purity and every element mass scales by that fraction. A 90%-pure 250 g sample of CaCO3 holds 90.09 g of calcium.

      Why don’t my element masses add up to the sample mass?

      Rounding. The unrounded values add up exactly; keep full precision until the last step and say which figures are rounded.

      How much fertilizer do I weigh for 10 g of elemental phosphorus?

      It depends on the label. From a 20% P2O5 fertilizer, which is 8.73% elemental P, you need 10 ÷ 0.0873 = 114.6 g. Never weigh the elemental target against the oxide percentage.

      How do I get element mass from a solution?

      Concentration × volume gives the compound mass first, then the element mass as usual. 250 mL of 2.00 M CaCl2 holds 55.489 g of CaCl2, hence 20.04 g of calcium. The Molarity calculator handles the first step.

      What does “also brings” mean in reverse mode?

      The other elements in the compound you weigh. Weighing 12.52 g of CuO for 10 g of copper also brings 2.52 g of oxygen — part of the weighed material, whether you wanted it or not.

      How does purity change the reverse calculation?

      Divide by the mass fraction times the purity. 100 g of calcium needs 100 ÷ (0.4004 × 0.90) = 277.5 g of 90%-pure CaCO3, against 249.7 g of the pure salt.
