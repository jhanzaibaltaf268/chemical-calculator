Element Mass Calculator

How much of each element is in the sample you weighed — and, in reverse, how much compound to weigh for a target amount of one element.

    This is percent composition multiplied by your sample mass. It is not the mass of a pure element from a mole count.

    The working

      The mass fraction is the part that does the work; the sample mass just scales it.

      Grams of each element

      Total

      Element masses must add back to the sample mass. That sum is the built-in check.

      Atoms in one formula unit

      One circle per atom, sized by atomic weight and coloured by the CPK convention used in molecular models. Hydrogen is often most of the atoms and very little of the mass.

      The method

      mass of element = sample mass × (atoms × atomic weight) ÷ molar mass. Reverse it by dividing instead: compound mass = target element mass ÷ mass fraction.

      Worked by hand: 250 g of CaCO3

      Atoms: Ca 1, C 1, O 3. Masses per mole: 40.078, 12.011, and 3 × 15.999 = 47.997. Molar mass 100.086 g/mol, so the fractions are Ca 40.04%, C 12.00%, O 47.96% — summing to exactly 100.00%.

      In 250 g: calcium 100.10 g, carbon 30.00 g, oxygen 119.90 g. Those add to 250.00 g, which is the check.

      Supplement labels. A 500 mg calcium carbonate tablet holds 500 × 0.40042 = about 200 mg of elemental calcium. The 500 mg is the weight of the salt. To get 200 mg of calcium you need 499.5 mg of the carbonate — which is why the two numbers on a label differ. The same trap applies to citrate and gluconate salts.

      Hydrates carry their water into every row

      CuSO4·5H2O is 249.677 g/mol. In 100 g there are 36.08 g of water, and the element rows count the water’s atoms too: Cu 25.45 g, S 12.84 g, H 4.04 g, O 57.67 g. Heating drives off the 36.08 g and leaves about 63.92 g of anhydrous salt.

      Traps worth knowing

      • Confusing atom count with mass share. CaCO3 is one fifth calcium by atom count and 40.04% by mass.
      • Forgetting bracket multipliers. (NH4)2 holds 2 N and 8 H.
      • Dropping hydrate water. Using CuSO4 for CuSO4·5H2O understates the molar mass by 36% and changes every row.
      • Reading a supplement label as salt mass. See the note above.
      • Applying purity twice. Scale once, and follow the certificate’s own definition.
      • Mixing mg and g. Convert before multiplying, not after.
      • An element that is not in the formula. Its mass is 0 g. That is an answer, not an error.

      Questions people actually ask

      How much calcium is in a 500 mg calcium carbonate tablet?

      About 200 mg of elemental calcium. Supplement labels usually state the elemental amount, not the weight of the salt.

      I need 100 g of calcium — how much CaCO3 do I weigh?

      Divide by the mass fraction: 100 ÷ 0.40042 = 249.7 g. Switch the calculator to reverse mode and it does this for any element.

      How does a hydrate change the answer?

      The water’s atoms count. In CuSO4·5H2O water is 36.08% of the mass, and the hydrogen and oxygen rows include it.

      What if my sample is not pure?

      Set the purity and every element mass scales by that fraction. A 90%-pure 250 g sample of CaCO3 holds 90.09 g of calcium.

      Why don’t my element masses add up to the sample mass?

      Rounding. The unrounded values add up exactly; keep full precision until the last step and say which figures are rounded.
