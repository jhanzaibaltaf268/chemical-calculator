    Ratio triage: snap, multiply, or stop

    Read the ratios after dividing by the smallest. What you do next depends on what you see — and one of the options is to stop.

    RatioMeaningAction
    1.00, 2.00, 3.00 (±0.03)Rounding noiseRound to the whole number.
    near .50HalvesMultiply every ratio by 2.
    near .33 or .67ThirdsMultiply every ratio by 3.
    near .25 or .75QuartersMultiply every ratio by 4.
    near .20, .40, .60, .80FifthsMultiply by 5, but check the data first.
    1.42, 2.71 …Not a clean ratioStop. A bigger multiplier does not rescue it — recheck the data, the atomic weights, a missing element, and purity.

    Worked by hand: Fe2O3

    A compound is 69.94% Fe and 30.06% O. Assume 100 g. Fe: 69.94 ÷ 55.845 = 1.2524 mol. O: 30.06 ÷ 15.999 = 1.8789 mol. Divide by 1.2524: Fe = 1.00, O = 1.5002.

    Rounding that 1.50 up gives FeO2, which is not the compound. Clear the fraction first — multiply both by 2 — and you get Fe2O3, 159.687 g/mol, which checks back at 69.94% Fe.

    Empirical to molecular: caffeine

    C 49.48%, H 5.19%, N 28.85%, O 16.48% gives whole-number ratios straight away: C 4, H 5, N 2, O 1. The empirical formula is C4H5N2O at 97.097 g/mol. Only the measured molar mass of 194.19 g/mol finishes the job: n = 194.19 ÷ 97.097 = 2.00, so the molecular formula is C8H10N4O2.

    C8H10N4O2 is the molecular formula, not the empirical one. Composition data alone can never reach it; if n is not close to a whole number, the molecular formula is undetermined and must be left that way.

    Hydrates and combustion

    Hydrate. Heat 2.50 g of CuSO4·xH2O to constant mass and 1.60 g of anhydrous salt remains, so 0.90 g of water left. Water: 0.90 ÷ 18.015 = 0.0500 mol. Salt: 1.60 ÷ 159.602 = 0.0100 mol. The ratio is 5.0, so x = 5. Treat H2O as one unit here — splitting it into H and O double-counts the oxygen.

    Combustion. 4.40 g CO2 gives 0.1000 mol C; 1.80 g H2O gives 2 × 0.0999 = 0.1998 mol H, because each water carries two hydrogens. The C:H part is CH2. Oxygen can only come from the mass difference against the original sample — never assume it.

    Traps worth knowing

    • Rounding 1.50 to 2 before multiplying. Clear fractions first, round last.
    • Rounding each ratio on its own. 1.33 and 2.67 become 1 and 3, destroying the ratio. Multiply both by 3 instead: 4 and 8.
    • Using percent directly as moles. Percent is mass. Divide by the atomic weight first.
    • Mixing percent rows with gram rows.
    • Assuming the missing percent is oxygen. If the rows total 85%, the rest could be an unmeasured element, water or an impurity. Say so rather than assigning it silently.
    • Forcing a non-integer n. If molar mass ÷ empirical mass is 5.4, something upstream is wrong.
    • Treating the empirical formula as an identification. CH2O is formaldehyde, acetic acid and glucose alike.

    Questions people actually ask

    What do I do if a ratio comes out as 1.5 or 1.33?

    Multiply every ratio by the same number: ×2 for halves, ×3 for thirds, ×4 for quarters. Never round one ratio on its own.

    What if the ratio is 1.42 or 2.71?

    Stop and recheck. Usual causes are a data error, a missing element, wrong atomic weights or an impure sample. A larger multiplier that forces whole numbers invents a formula.

    What if the percentages do not add to 100?

    The ratio can still be computed, but the analysis is incomplete. Oxygen is the usual missing element — say so explicitly rather than assigning the remainder silently.

    How do I get the molecular formula?

    n = molar mass ÷ empirical formula mass, then multiply every subscript by n. For CH2O at 30.026 g/mol with a measured 180 g/mol, n = 6 and the formula is C6H12O6.

    Can the empirical and molecular formulas be the same?

    Yes, when n = 1 — water, carbon dioxide, formaldehyde. For ionic compounds such as NaCl or Fe2O3, the empirical formula is the formula; there is no separate molecular one.

    Why do different compounds share one empirical formula?

    Empirical means ratio only. CH2O fits formaldehyde, acetic acid and glucose equally well. Molar mass and structure data are what separate them.
