      The same amount under every standard

      Worked by hand: 2.50 mol of N2, four answers

      Textbook STP: 2.50 × 22.414 = 56.04 L. IUPAC STP: 2.50 × 22.711 = 56.78 L. SATP: 2.50 × 24.790 = 61.97 L. At 25 °C and 1 atm: 61.16 L. Every one is correct for its own definition, and the full gas law agrees with the shortcut: V = nRT/P = (2.50 × 0.082057 × 273.15) ÷ 1 = 56.04 L.

      The last two rows are both at 25 °C. The 0.81 L between them is only 1 atm against 1 bar — which is exactly why the standard has to be stated.

      Worked by hand: a measurement back to STP

      You collect 250 mL of N2 at 22 °C and 0.98 atm. Convert first: 0.250 L, 295.15 K. Then n = PV ÷ RT = (0.98 × 0.250) ÷ (0.082057 × 295.15) = 0.01012 mol. At textbook STP that same gas occupies 0.01012 × 22.414 = 0.2267 L, about 227 mL. It shrank, because cooling to 0 °C outweighs the small pressure rise — a sanity check worth doing every time.

      The same litre, wildly different masses

      One litre at textbook STP holds 1 ÷ 22.414 = 0.0446 mol of any ideal gas — but the mass in that litre depends entirely on which gas it is. CO2: 0.0446 × 44.009 = 1.96 g. H2: 0.0446 × 2.016 = 0.090 g. Same volume, 21.8 times the mass. Molar volume is universal; density is not.

      1 L of H₂ at STP — 0.090 g

      Identical volumes, 21.8-fold mass gap: molar mass does the work.

      From a weighed sample to litres

      The moles in a gas problem usually come from a weighed sample first: 44 g of CO2 is 44 ÷ 44.009 = 0.9998 mol, effectively 1.00 mol, which fills 1.00 × 22.414 = 22.41 L at textbook STP. The moles-from-mass step is the Grams to Moles calculator’s job; this page takes over once moles are known.

      Which STP, stated three ways

      A question can mean textbook STP (0 °C, 1 atm, 22.414 L/mol), IUPAC STP (0 °C, 1 bar, 22.711 L/mol), or SATP mislabelled as STP (25 °C, 1 bar, 24.790 L/mol). For 2.50 mol of N2 those give 56.04, 56.78 and 61.97 L — a spread of 5.93 L, 10.6%, from the same letters. Read the question’s pressure and temperature words, not just its initials.

      The twelvefold Kelvin trap, worked

      One mole at 25 °C and 1 atm: V = 1 × 0.082057 × 298.15 ÷ 1 = 24.47 L. Put 25 in place of 298.15 and you get 1 × 0.082057 × 25 = 2.05 L — 11.93 times too small, about twelvefold. The gas law wants kelvin, always; the Celsius number is not a temperature for this equation, it is a label.

      Traps worth knowing

      • Using 22.414 away from 0 °C. At 25 °C and 1 atm it is 24.465 L/mol — the shortcut understates the volume by about 8%.
      • Mixing the two STPs. 22.414 against 22.711 is 1.3%, enough to lose a mark.
      • Calling 25 °C and 100 kPa “STP”. That is SATP, 24.790 L/mol.
      • Putting °C into PV = nRT. Always kelvin.
      • Atomic masses for diatomic gases. O2 is 31.998 g/mol, N2 is 28.014, H2 is 2.016 — not the atomic values.
      • Applying the gas shortcut to a solution. Dissolved moles do not occupy 22.414 L. Use litres = moles ÷ molarity.
      • Assuming litres tell you mass. One mole of H2 and one of CO2 fill the same 22.414 L, but CO2 is about 1.96 g/L against 0.09 g/L — roughly 22 times heavier.
      • Treating the ideal gas law as exact. It is a good estimate at low pressure and well above the boiling point. For compressed gas, or vapours near condensation such as ammonia or water, treat the answer as an estimate.
      • Forgetting water vapour in a collected gas. A gas collected over water at 25 °C and 1 atm total pressure is not at 101.325 kPa of dry gas: water vapour contributes 3.17 kPa, so the dry pressure is 101.325 − 3.17 = 98.155 kPa. Use the dry pressure in PV = nRT.
      • Correcting temperature but not pressure. Converting a measurement back to STP needs both corrections. Cooling alone, or pressurising alone, leaves half the error in place — the worked measurement above corrects 22 °C to 0 °C and 0.98 atm to 1 atm together.

      Questions people actually ask

      How many litres is one mole of gas at STP?

      22.414 L at textbook STP (0 °C, 1 atm); 22.711 L at IUPAC STP (0 °C, 1 bar). Check which your question means.

      Why is my textbook 22.4 L when this says 22.7 L?

      Neither is wrong. 22.414 L uses 1 atm (101.325 kPa); 22.711 L uses 1 bar (100 kPa). Same temperature, different pressure.

      Can I use 22.4 L/mol at room temperature?

      No — only at 0 °C. At 25 °C and 1 atm use 24.465 L/mol, or the full gas law.

      What is the difference between STP and SATP?

      STP is at 0 °C; SATP is at 25 °C and 1 bar, giving 24.790 L/mol.

      Is one mole of CO2 the same volume as one mole of helium?

      For an ideal gas at the same temperature and pressure, yes. They differ in mass and density: about 1.96 g/L against 0.18 g/L at STP.

      How do I convert a weighed sample to litres?

      Mass ÷ molar mass gives moles, then moles × molar volume gives litres. 44 g of CO2 is 0.9998 mol, effectively 1.00 mol, which fills 22.41 L at textbook STP. The Grams to Moles calculator does the first step.

      What if my gas was collected over water?

      Subtract the water vapour pressure first. At 25 °C and 1 atm total, the dry gas is at 101.325 − 3.17 = 98.155 kPa. Use that dry pressure in PV = nRT, not the total.

      Why does using °C instead of K shrink the answer twelvefold?

      Because the gas law scales with absolute temperature: 298.15 K against the 25 you typed, a ratio of 11.93. One mole at 25 °C and 1 atm is 24.47 L with kelvin, 2.05 L with the Celsius number.

      How much heavier is a litre of CO2 than a litre of H2?

      At textbook STP, 1.96 g against 0.090 g — 21.8 times heavier, at identical volume. Same moles per litre, very different molar masses.
