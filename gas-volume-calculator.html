      The same amount under every standard

      Worked by hand: 2.50 mol of N2, four answers

      Textbook STP: 2.50 × 22.414 = 56.04 L. IUPAC STP: 2.50 × 22.711 = 56.78 L. SATP: 2.50 × 24.790 = 61.97 L. At 25 °C and 1 atm: 61.16 L. Every one is correct for its own definition, and the full gas law agrees with the shortcut: V = nRT/P = (2.50 × 0.082057 × 273.15) ÷ 1 = 56.04 L.

      The last two rows are both at 25 °C. The 0.81 L between them is only 1 atm against 1 bar — which is exactly why the standard has to be stated.

      Worked by hand: a measurement back to STP

      You collect 250 mL of N2 at 22 °C and 0.98 atm. Convert first: 0.250 L, 295.15 K. Then n = PV ÷ RT = (0.98 × 0.250) ÷ (0.082057 × 295.15) = 0.01012 mol. At textbook STP that same gas occupies 0.01012 × 22.414 = 0.2267 L, about 227 mL. It shrank, because cooling to 0 °C outweighs the small pressure rise — a sanity check worth doing every time.

      Traps worth knowing

      • Using 22.414 away from 0 °C. At 25 °C and 1 atm it is 24.465 L/mol — the shortcut understates the volume by about 8%.
      • Mixing the two STPs. 22.414 against 22.711 is 1.3%, enough to lose a mark.
      • Calling 25 °C and 100 kPa “STP”. That is SATP, 24.790 L/mol.
      • Putting °C into PV = nRT. Always kelvin.
      • Atomic masses for diatomic gases. O2 is 31.998 g/mol, N2 is 28.014, H2 is 2.016 — not the atomic values.
      • Applying the gas shortcut to a solution. Dissolved moles do not occupy 22.414 L. Use litres = moles ÷ molarity.
      • Assuming litres tell you mass. One mole of H2 and one of CO2 fill the same 22.414 L, but CO2 is about 1.96 g/L against 0.09 g/L — roughly 22 times heavier.
      • Treating the ideal gas law as exact. It is a good estimate at low pressure and well above the boiling point. For compressed gas, or vapours near condensation such as ammonia or water, treat the answer as an estimate.

      Questions people actually ask

      How many litres is one mole of gas at STP?

      22.414 L at textbook STP (0 °C, 1 atm); 22.711 L at IUPAC STP (0 °C, 1 bar). Check which your question means.

      Why is my textbook 22.4 L when this says 22.7 L?

      Neither is wrong. 22.414 L uses 1 atm (101.325 kPa); 22.711 L uses 1 bar (100 kPa). Same temperature, different pressure.

      Can I use 22.4 L/mol at room temperature?

      No — only at 0 °C. At 25 °C and 1 atm use 24.465 L/mol, or the full gas law.

      What is the difference between STP and SATP?

      STP is at 0 °C; SATP is at 25 °C and 1 bar, giving 24.790 L/mol.

      Is one mole of CO2 the same volume as one mole of helium?

      For an ideal gas at the same temperature and pressure, yes. They differ in mass and density: about 1.96 g/L against 0.18 g/L at STP.
