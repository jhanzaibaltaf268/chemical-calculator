Grams to Moles Calculator

Mass to moles and back — the molar mass does the work, and the molecule count comes free.

Names work too: glucose, aspirin, washing soda.

Try:
—

    Moles = grams ÷ molar mass. Grams = moles × molar mass. One mole holds 6.02214076 × 1023 formula units.

    The working

      The molar mass line is the whole calculation. Get the form right — hydrate included — and the rest is one division.

      Moles at a glance

      Two directions, one divisor

      moles = grams ÷ molar mass and grams = moles × molar mass. The molar mass comes from the formula, brackets and hydrate water included.

      Worked by hand: 25 g of sodium chloride

      NaCl weighs 22.990 + 35.45 = 58.440 g/mol.

      1. Divide. 25 ÷ 58.440 = 0.4278 mol.
      2. Count the units. 0.4278 × 6.02214076 × 1023 = 2.58 × 1023 formula units.

      The default above runs the other way round: 180.156 g of glucose ÷ 180.156 g/mol = 1.0000 mol exactly — one mole of glucose by definition.

      Worked by hand: 180.156 g of glucose is one mole

      Glucose, C6H12O6, weighs 6 × 12.011 + 12 × 1.008 + 6 × 15.999 = 72.066 + 12.096 + 95.994 = 180.156 g/mol.

      1. Divide. 180.156 ÷ 180.156 = 1.0000 mol.
      2. Count the molecules. 1.0000 × 6.02214076 × 1023 = 6.022 × 1023 glucose molecules.

      That is why the calculator opens on 180.156 g of glucose: the weighed mass and the molar mass are the same number, so the answer is one mole by construction.

      Worked by hand: 250 mg of aspirin, and the reverse check

      Aspirin, C9H8O4, weighs 9 × 12.011 + 8 × 1.008 + 4 × 15.999 = 108.099 + 8.064 + 63.996 = 180.159 g/mol.

      1. Convert milligrams first. 250 mg = 0.250 g.
      2. Divide. 0.250 ÷ 180.159 = 0.001388 mol, which is 1.388 mmol (multiply moles by 1,000).
      3. Count the molecules. 0.001388 × 6.02214076 × 1023 = 8.359 × 1020 aspirin molecules.
      4. Check in reverse. 0.001388 mol × 180.159 g/mol = 0.250 g — the starting mass, as it must be.

      Every moles question gives you something and asks for something else. Match what you have to the right rearrangement, and the arithmetic is one step:

      Units on both ladders at once

      Balances read milligrams, grams or kilograms; protocols ask for moles, millimoles or micromoles. Cross both ladders in one chain, converting mass to grams first and moles to the wanted unit last. Watch 250 mg of aspirin travel the whole way: 250 mg = 0.250 g, 0.250 ÷ 180.159 = 0.001388 mol, which is 1.388 mmol, which is 1,388 µmol. The same chain the other way: a balance reading 125 mg of NaCl is 0.125 g, 0.125 ÷ 58.440 = 0.002139 mol, or 2.139 mmol. And a bulk scale reading 2.5 kg of water is 2,500 g, 2,500 ÷ 18.015 = 138.77 mol. One chain, no separate conversions to remember.

      250 mg→0.250 g→0.001388 mol→1.388 mmol→1,388 µmol

      Same mass, different moles

      One hundred grams of three substances holds very different amounts. Water gives the most moles because each molecule is lightest; glucose gives the fewest. Bars are scaled to water.

      Water — 100 g ÷ 18.015 = 5.551 mol

      Salt — 100 g ÷ 58.440 = 1.711 mol

      Glucose — 100 g ÷ 180.156 = 0.5551 mol

      Same weighed mass, ten times fewer moles of glucose than of water. There is no single grams-to-moles factor: the substance sets it.

      Using the moles: ratios in a reaction

      Converting to moles is usually only the first half. Balanced equations speak in moles, so the mole counts go straight into the ratio. Worked end to end: hydrogen and oxygen make water, 2H2 + O2 → 2H2O, a 2:1 ratio of hydrogen to oxygen. Start with 10 g of hydrogen: H2 weighs 2.016 g/mol, so 10 ÷ 2.016 = 4.960 mol of H2. The ratio halves it for oxygen: 4.960 ÷ 2 = 2.480 mol of O2, which weighs 2.480 × 31.998 = 79.36 g. The water made is 4.960 mol × 18.015 g/mol = 89.36 g. Mass in, moles across, mass out — the ratio step in the middle is why the conversion exists at all.

      Impure samples and mixtures, worked

      Purity first, division second. A 100 g bottle that is 95% NaCl holds 0.95 × 100 = 95 g of NaCl, which is 95 ÷ 58.440 = 1.626 mol; dividing the full 100 g would overstate the moles by the 5 g of impurity. A mixture has no single molar mass at all: work each component separately. Ten grams of a 50/50 NaCl/KCl mix is 5 g of NaCl, 5 ÷ 58.440 = 0.08556 mol, plus 5 g of KCl, 5 ÷ 74.548 = 0.06707 mol. Two mole counts, not one — adding the masses and dividing once by either molar mass gives a number that means nothing.

      How precise should the answer be?

      Match your least precise input. A balance reading to the nearest 0.01 g does not justify five decimals in the mole count, and a molar mass quoted to three decimals caps the answer at three or four significant figures. The calculator shows more digits than most measurements justify so the arithmetic stays visible; round the final answer to what you actually measured. Rounding changes the display, not the calculation — keep full precision through the steps and round once, at the end.

      Try it yourself

      1. How many moles are in 40 g of glucose?
      2. A protocol asks for 2.5 mmol of NaCl. What mass do you weigh?
      3. You have 5 g of aspirin at 80% purity. How many moles of aspirin is that?

      Answers: (1) 40 ÷ 180.156 = 0.2220 mol. (2) 0.0025 mol × 58.440 g/mol = 0.1461 g, or 146.1 mg. (3) 0.80 × 5 = 4 g of aspirin, 4 ÷ 180.159 = 0.02220 mol.

      Traps worth knowing

      1. Which form? Anhydrous CuSO4 is 159.602 g/mol; the pentahydrate is 249.677. Typing the wrong one moves every answer by 56%.
      2. Salt or free base? A drug·HCl salt weighs about 36.458 g/mol more than the free base. Divide by the salt mass when you weigh the salt.
      3. Milligrams are not grams. 250 mg is 0.250 g. Dividing milligrams by g/mol gives millimoles, not moles.
      4. Moles of what? State the unit: moles of NaCl formula units, moles of glucose molecules. “Moles of sodium” in NaCl is the same number; in Na2CO3 it is double. For grams of a single element inside a compound, use the Element Mass calculator.
      5. Round once. Keep full precision until the final line.
      6. Brackets multiply everything inside. Ca(OH)2 is 74.092 g/mol, not 57.085: the subscript 2 doubles both O and H. Count the bracketed group twice.
      7. Purity counts. A 95% pure sample contributes only 0.95 × the weighed mass of compound. Multiply by the purity fraction before dividing.
      8. Moles are not molarity. Moles alone say nothing about concentration; divide by the volume in litres to get mol/L — the Molarity calculator does that step.
      9. Millimoles are not micromoles. One millimole is 1,000 micromoles. Reporting 1.388 mmol as 1.388 µmol understates the amount a thousandfold — check which side of the ladder the protocol wants.
      10. More digits than the balance justifies. A mass weighed to two significant figures gives a mole count to two significant figures. Extra digits from the calculator are display, not accuracy.
      The hydrate is the expensive one. Forgetting five waters in CuSO4·5H2O understates the molar mass by 90.075 g/mol, so the mole count comes out about 56% too high. Check the label before the arithmetic.

      Questions people actually ask

      How do I convert grams to moles?

      Divide the mass in grams by the molar mass in g/mol. 25 g of NaCl ÷ 58.440 g/mol = 0.4278 mol.

      How do I convert moles back to grams?

      Multiply moles by the molar mass. 2 mol of glucose × 180.156 g/mol = 360.312 g.

      How many molecules are in a mole?

      6.02214076 × 1023 — the Avogadro constant. Multiply moles by it for molecules, or by it for formula units of a salt.

      Do I use the hydrate or the anhydrous mass?

      Whichever form you actually weigh. The label decides: a hydrate’s waters are part of the weighed mass and part of the molar mass.

      Why does my mole count look 1,000× off?

      Almost always milligrams entered as grams, or the reverse. 250 mg is 0.250 g; dividing 250 by g/mol gives millimoles.

      How many moles are in 1 gram of water?

      1 ÷ 18.015 g/mol = 0.05551 mol, about 3.343 × 1022 water molecules.

      What is a millimole, and when do I use it?

      One millimole (mmol) is 0.001 mol. Use it for milligram-scale work: 250 mg of aspirin is 1.388 mmol, which is easier to read than 0.001388 mol.

      How many grams is 0.5 mol of sodium chloride?

      Multiply: 0.5 mol × 58.440 g/mol = 29.220 g.

      How many moles of oxygen atoms are in 1 mol of glucose?

      Six. Each glucose molecule, C6H12O6, carries 6 oxygen atoms, so 1 mol of glucose contains 6 mol of oxygen atoms.

      I know the mass and the moles. How do I find the molar mass?

      Divide: M = m ÷ n. A sample weighing 36.04 g that contains 2.000 mol has a molar mass of 36.04 ÷ 2.000 = 18.02 g/mol — water, within rounding. This rearrangement identifies an unknown or checks an experimental measurement.

      How do I get moles from a solution?

      Multiply strength by volume: n = M × V, with volume in litres. 0.200 L of 0.500 M solution holds 0.100 mol. The Molarity calculator carries this step, including the mass to weigh out.

      How do I get moles from a gas?

      Use n = PV ÷ RT, with pressure, volume and temperature in matching units. The Gas Volume calculator handles the gas side, including which STP value the question means.

      What if my sample is only 95% pure?

      Multiply the weighed mass by the purity fraction first, then divide. 100 g at 95% purity is 95 g of compound: 95 ÷ 58.440 = 1.626 mol of NaCl. Dividing the full 100 g counts the impurity as product.

      How many decimals should I report?

      As many as your least precise input justifies — usually three or four significant figures. Keep full precision through the working and round once, at the end.
