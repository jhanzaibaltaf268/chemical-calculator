    How the number is worked out

    Chemical weight is the mass of one mole of a formula unit, in grams per mole. Most textbooks call it molar mass; for ionic salts such as NaCl the exact term is formula weight. The arithmetic is the same in every case, and so is the number.

    1. Expand the formula. A subscript after a closing bracket multiplies everything inside it. A hydrate dot starts a second part, multiplied by its leading number.
    2. Count the atoms of each element.
    3. Multiply each count by that element’s standard atomic weight.
    4. Add the subtotals. Round once, at the end.

    Worked by hand: potassium ferrocyanide, K4[Fe(CN)6]

    The nested bracket is what makes this one worth doing on paper. The 6 multiplies the whole CN group; the 4 outside the square bracket applies to potassium only.

    Which formula to type, by what the bottle says

    Typing the wrong form of the right compound is the most common error in lab work, and the calculator cannot catch it. The label decides.

    LabelTypeWhy it matters
    Anhydrous salt (CuSO4)CuSO4159.602 g/mol. Typing the hydrate instead overstates it by 56%.
    Hydrate (CuSO4·5H2O)CuSO4·5H2O249.677 g/mol. The five waters are 90.075 g/mol, about 36% of the total.
    Salt form (drug·HCl)The salt, not the free baseHCl adds 36.458 g/mol — roughly a 15% shift for many drugs.
    Gas (O2, N2)O231.998 g/mol. A single O is half that.
    Reaction coefficient (2H2O)H2OCoefficients count moles, not atoms inside one molecule. They never enter the weight.
    Ion (SO42−)SO4The charge changes neither the atom count nor the weight.
    Why another calculator may disagree with this one. Almost always the atomic-weight table or the rounding rule. Water is 18.015 g/mol here and 18.016 with O = 16.00; sulfuric acid is 98.072 against 98.086. A gap of 0.01 to 0.07 g/mol is an edition effect, not chemistry. For mass spectrometry, use monoisotopic mass instead of standard average weight.

    Traps worth knowing

    • Co is not CO. Cobalt is 58.933 g/mol; carbon monoxide is 28.010. A capital starts a new element, and a lowercase letter belongs to the element before it.
    • A bracket subscript multiplies everything inside. Al2(SO4)3 holds 2 Al, 3 S and 12 O.
    • The hydrate dot is not a decimal point. Leaving the waters out of CuSO4·5H2O loses 90.075 g/mol.
    • Atomic number is not atomic weight. Sodium is element 11 and weighs 22.990 g/mol.
    • Diatomic gases are written as pairs. H2, N2, O2, F2, Cl2, Br2, I2.
    • Average is not monoisotopic. Chlorine’s 35.45 g/mol averages about 76% Cl-35 with 24% Cl-37.
    • Round once. Keep full precision until the final line.
    • Millilitres are not litres. 250 mL of 0.500 M NaOH needs 0.125 mol × 39.997 = 5.00 g, dissolved and made up to 250 mL.
    • Some elements have no standard atomic weight. Many radioactive and synthetic ones do not; this calculator says so rather than inventing a value.

    Questions people actually ask

    How do you calculate chemical weight from a formula?

    Multiply each element’s atomic weight by its atom count, counting atoms inside brackets and hydrate water, then add the results. The unit is g/mol.

    Is chemical weight the same as molar mass and molecular weight?

    The numbers are identical for the same formula and the same atomic weights. Strictly, molecular weight is a dimensionless ratio, molar mass is in g/mol, and formula weight is the term for ionic salts such as NaCl.

    Why does my answer differ by 0.01 to 0.07 g/mol from another source?

    The atomic-weight table or the rounding rule, nearly every time. Check which values the other source uses before assuming either is wrong.

    How do I enter CuSO₄·5H₂O?

    Type the full formula with the dot, or an asterisk or full stop if that is easier. The answer is 249.677 g/mol, not the anhydrous 159.602.

    Do parentheses and brackets change the answer?

    Yes. A subscript after a closing bracket multiplies every atom inside it. K₄[Fe(CN)₆] holds 6 C and 6 N, and weighs 368.345 g/mol.

    Does capitalisation matter?

    Yes. Co is cobalt and CO is carbon monoxide. Entering one for the other gives a completely different answer, so the calculator never silently fixes case.

    How many grams do I weigh for a solution?

    Grams = molarity × volume in litres × molar mass. For 250 mL of 0.500 M NaOH: 0.500 × 0.250 × 39.997 = 5.00 g, dissolved and made up to 250 mL.

    What is percent composition for?

    Each element’s subtotal divided by the total, times 100. It checks a compound’s identity against elemental analysis, and gives the element masses in any sample.
