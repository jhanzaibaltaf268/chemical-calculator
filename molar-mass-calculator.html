      How the number is worked out

      Molar mass is the mass of one mole of a formula unit, in grams per mole. The arithmetic never changes: expand, count, multiply, add. Round once, at the end.

      1. Expand the formula. A subscript after a closing bracket multiplies everything inside it. A hydrate dot starts a second part, multiplied by its leading number.
      2. Count the atoms of each element.
      3. Multiply each count by that element’s standard atomic weight.
      4. Add the subtotals.

      Worked by hand: glucose, C6H12O6

      ElementAtomsAtomic weightSubtotal
      C612.01172.066
      H121.00812.096
      O615.99995.994
      Total24180.156

      So glucose is 180.156 g/mol = 0.180156 kg/mol = 180.156 Da. The same routine gives sulfuric acid, H2SO4: (2 × 1.008) + 32.06 + (4 × 15.999) = 98.072 g/mol.

      Worked by hand: calcium hydroxide, Ca(OH)2

      Expand the bracket first: (OH)2 means 2 O and 2 H, plus the single Ca outside it.

      ElementAtomsAtomic weightSubtotal
      Ca140.07840.078
      O215.99931.998
      H21.0082.016
      Total574.092

      So calcium hydroxide is 74.092 g/mol: 40.078 + 31.998 + 2.016 = 74.092. Counting only one OH by mistake gives 57.085 g/mol, which is 17.007 short — exactly one OH group.

      Worked by hand: potassium hexacyanoferrate(II), K4[Fe(CN)6]

      Work from the inside out: (CN)6 means 6 C and 6 N, inside a square bracket holding one Fe, with 4 K outside it.

      ElementAtomsAtomic weightSubtotal
      K439.098156.392
      Fe155.84555.845
      C612.01172.066
      N614.00784.042
      Total17368.345

      So K4[Fe(CN)6] is 368.345 g/mol: 156.392 + 55.845 + 72.066 + 84.042 = 368.345, or 0.368345 kg/mol.

      Reading a formula: what the calculator accepts

      Symbols start with a capital letter, with a lower-case second letter where the element has one. Na is sodium; NA will not parse, because the parser reads each capital as the start of a new element. A number straight after a symbol counts that element only. A number after a closing bracket multiplies everything inside it, so Ca3(PO4)2 holds 3 Ca, 2 P and 8 O. Groups can nest: work from the inside out, as in the K4[Fe(CN)6] example above. Hydrates take a dot, an asterisk or a full stop plus a leading number: CuSO4·5H2O, Na2CO3.10H2O. Spaces are ignored. An unknown symbol or an unbalanced bracket gives an error message, not a wrong number.

      Units: g/mol, kg/mol, daltons — and mg/mmol

      Grams per mole is the lab unit. Kilograms per mole divides by 1,000. A dalton (Da) carries the same number as g/mol, and is the usual unit for large molecules, quoted in kilodaltons. Milligrams per millimole carries the same number again, which suits small-scale work: 1 g/mol = 1 mg/mmol.

      From molar mass to moles, grams, and molecules

      Three relations cover nearly every conversion: moles = mass ÷ molar mass, mass = moles × molar mass, and molecules = moles × 6.02214076 × 1023, Avogadro’s number, fixed exactly since the 2019 redefinition of the mole. Worked: 36 g of glucose is 36 ÷ 180.156 = 0.1998 mol, which is 0.1998 × 6.02214076 × 1023 = 1.203 × 1023 molecules. The other way: 0.5 mol of sodium chloride weighs 0.5 × 58.440 = 29.22 g. The Grams to Moles calculator does both directions with the molecule count alongside.

      Hydrates: which mass does your bottle need?

      The label on the bottle decides, not the name of the salt. Anhydrous copper(II) sulfate is 159.602 g/mol; the pentahydrate on the shelf is 249.677 g/mol, because the five waters add 5 × 18.015 = 90.075 g/mol — 36.08% of the hydrated mass. Washing soda is the same trap at larger scale: anhydrous Na2CO3 is 105.988 g/mol, the decahydrate Na2CO3·10H2O is 286.138 g/mol. A recipe calling for 1 mol of sodium carbonate needs 286.138 g of the decahydrate; weighing 105.988 g of it gives only 105.988 ÷ 286.138 = 0.370 mol, barely more than a third of what was meant. The dot number need not be whole: plaster, CaSO4·½H2O, is 136.134 + 9.0075 = 145.142 g/mol. Heating a hydrate drives the water off, so the mass lost on full heating is the water share — 36.08% for copper(II) sulfate pentahydrate.

      Isotopes and labelled compounds

      Standard atomic weights are natural-abundance averages, which is what weighing and ordinary stoichiometry need. A labelled sample is a different substance for mass purposes. Heavy water, D2O, is 2 × 2.014 + 15.999 = 20.027 g/mol, not 18.015, because each deuterium weighs 2.014. Glucose with all six carbons as carbon-13 shifts its molecular ion from 180 to 186, one unit per carbon. Use the average table for weighing a bottle; use exact isotopic masses for mass spectrometry, NMR tracers, and any work where individual isotopes are resolved.

      Molar mass from a gas experiment

      When the formula is unknown but the substance is a gas, the ideal gas law gives the molar mass: M = mRT ÷ PV, with m in grams, R = 0.082057 L·atm/(mol·K), T in kelvin, P in atm, V in litres. Worked: 2 g of an unknown gas at 1 atm, 273.15 K, in 1 L gives n = (1 × 1) ÷ (0.082057 × 273.15) = 0.04462 mol, so M = 2 ÷ 0.04462 = 44.83 g/mol — near carbon dioxide at 44.009, but nearness is not identity, and a second test is needed before naming the gas. The method is only as good as the ideal-gas approximation: light gases, low pressure, near room temperature. At high pressure real gases deviate and the answer drifts.

      Percent composition and the empirical formula

      Each element’s mass share is its subtotal divided by the total, times 100, and the shares always add to 100 — a sum of 97% or 103% means something went wrong. The shares also run backwards into a formula: combustion analysis reporting 40.0% C, 6.7% H and 53.3% O gives, per 100 g, 40.0 ÷ 12.011 = 3.33 mol C, 6.7 ÷ 1.008 = 6.65 mol H and 53.3 ÷ 15.999 = 3.33 mol O, a ratio of 1:2:1, so the empirical formula is CH2O at 30.026 g/mol. The same percentage does not mean the same compound: glucose, C6H12O6, shares the 1:2:1 ratio with formaldehyde, CH2O. Dividing a measured molecular mass by the empirical mass picks the multiple: 180.156 ÷ 30.026 = 6. The Percent Composition and Empirical Formula calculators carry both directions.

      Why another table gives a slightly different number

      IUPAC revises its weights, so tables from different years disagree in the second or third decimal. Older tables print C = 12.0107 and H = 1.00794, which makes water 18.016 g/mol where this page gives 18.015. A few elements have no single value at all: lithium ranges from 6.938 to 6.997 and sulfur from 32.059 to 32.076 depending on the source of the sample, so a table must pick a representative figure. This page uses one canonical set across every calculation. A gap in the last decimals is an edition effect, not chemistry; check the other source’s table before assuming either side is wrong.

      Ions, mixtures, and polymers

      A charge adds or removes electrons, at 0.00055 g/mol each. Neutral H3NSi is 3.024 + 14.007 + 28.085 = 45.116 g/mol; as the ion (H3NSi)+ it is 45.116 − 0.00055 = 45.115 g/mol, a difference below the last digit most tables print, so ordinary weighing ignores it. A mixture has no single molar mass either: take the mole-fraction weighted average, so a 50/50 mix of N2 (28.014) and O2 (31.998) averages 30.006 g/mol. Polymers work the same way by construction: the repeating unit C2H4 is 28.054 g/mol, so a chain of 1,000 units averages 28,054 g/mol, and real samples quote a number-average or weight-average because chains are never all the same length.

      How precise should the answer be?

      Match the measurement, not the table. Weighing to ±0.01 g justifies two decimals, not five; most everyday calculations need only three or four significant figures; analytical work uses full precision and rounds only the final answer. This calculator rounds once, at the end, for the same reason: rounding each subtotal first lets small errors accumulate, and in a large molecule they reach a full g/mol.

      Try it yourself

      1. How many moles are in 25 g of K4[Fe(CN)6]?
      2. You weigh 50 g of washing soda decahydrate, Na2CO3·10H2O. How many moles is that, and how many grams of Na2CO3 are inside it?
      3. An unknown gas: 2 g at 1 atm, 273.15 K, in 1 L. What is its molar mass?

      Answers: (1) 25 ÷ 368.345 = 0.06787 mol. (2) 50 ÷ 286.138 = 0.1747 mol, containing 50 × (105.988 ÷ 286.138) = 18.52 g of Na2CO3. (3) 44.83 g/mol.

      How big is a mole, really?

      A mole is a count, not a size — 6.02214076 × 1023 of anything. The count is so large that everyday comparisons break in entertaining ways. Count one atom per second and reaching a mole takes 1.91 × 1016 years, about 1.4 million times the age of the universe. Stack a mole of ordinary paper sheets, each 0.1 mm thick, and the pile reaches about 6,400 light-years. And a mole of water is the most ordinary thing of all: 18.015 g, about 18 mL, just over a tablespoon.

      Molar mass in the kitchen and the medicine cabinet

      Spoon measures are rough, but they make the numbers tangible. A tablespoon of water, about 15 g, is about 0.83 mol. A level teaspoon of table salt, about 6 g, is about 0.10 mol. A teaspoon of sugar, about 4 g, is about 0.012 mol of sucrose. A teaspoon of baking soda, about 5 g, is about 0.060 mol. And one ordinary 325 mg aspirin tablet is 0.325 ÷ 180.159 = 0.0018 mol of aspirin. Same unit, same arithmetic, from the kitchen to the lab bench.

      Traps worth knowing

      1. Co is not CO. Cobalt is 58.933 g/mol; carbon monoxide is 28.010. A capital starts a new element.
      2. A bracket subscript multiplies everything inside. Al2(SO4)3 holds 2 Al, 3 S and 12 O.
      3. The hydrate dot is not a decimal point. Leaving the waters out of CuSO4·5H2O loses 90.075 g/mol.
      4. Average is not monoisotopic. Chlorine’s 35.45 g/mol averages about 76% Cl-35 with 24% Cl-37. Mass spectrometry wants the monoisotopic figure instead.
      5. Round once. Keep full precision until the final line; rounding each subtotal first drifts the total.
      6. Some elements have no standard atomic weight. Many radioactive and synthetic ones do not; this calculator says so rather than inventing a value.
      7. Isomers share a molar mass. Glucose and fructose are both C6H12O6, so both are 180.156 g/mol — the number alone cannot tell them apart.
      8. Atom count is not mass share. Water is two-thirds hydrogen by atom count but only 11.19% hydrogen by mass (2.016 out of 18.015).
      9. Hydrogen is not H₂. One H atom is 1.008 g/mol; hydrogen gas, H2, is 2.016. Using the element where the question means the gas halves the answer.
      10. Mass is not molar mass. 18.015 g of water is a mass; 18.015 g/mol is the mass of a mole of it. The numbers matching does not make them the same quantity.
      11. Atomic number is not atomic weight. Carbon’s box shows 6, the proton count, and 12.011, the weight. Using 6 halves every carbon contribution.
      Why another calculator may disagree by 0.01 to 0.07 g/mol. Almost always the atomic-weight table or the rounding rule. Water is 18.015 g/mol here and 18.016 with O = 16.00. A gap that small is an edition effect, not chemistry.

      Questions people actually ask

      Is molar mass the same as molecular weight?

      The numbers are identical for the same formula and the same atomic weights. Molar mass carries the unit g/mol; molecular weight is strictly a dimensionless ratio, and formula weight is the term for ionic salts such as NaCl.

      When do I use kg/mol?

      When the quantity scales up: divide g/mol by 1,000. Glucose is 180.156 g/mol = 0.180156 kg/mol. One kilomole of glucose weighs 180.156 kg.

      How do I enter a hydrate?

      Type the full formula with the dot, or an asterisk or full stop. CuSO4·5H2O is 249.677 g/mol, not the anhydrous 159.602.

      Do parentheses and brackets change the answer?

      Yes. A subscript after a closing bracket multiplies every atom inside it. K4[Fe(CN)6] holds 6 C and 6 N, and weighs 368.345 g/mol.

      Why does my answer differ slightly from another source?

      The atomic-weight table or the rounding rule, nearly every time. Check which values the other source uses before assuming either is wrong.

      What is a dalton, and when is it used?

      A dalton (Da) is the unit of molecular mass, and its number equals the molar mass in g/mol: glucose is 180.156 Da. Daltons are the usual unit for large molecules — the Protein Molecular Weight calculator quotes proteins in kilodaltons (kDa), thousands of daltons.

      Can two different compounds have the same molar mass?

      Yes, when they share a formula: isomers such as glucose and fructose are both C6H12O6 and both 180.156 g/mol. Unrelated formulas can also land close together, so always check the formula, not just the number.

      Should I use the average or the monoisotopic mass?

      Use the average (standard atomic weight) mass for weighing and ordinary stoichiometry — that is what this calculator gives. Use the monoisotopic mass, built from the lightest isotope of each element, for mass spectrometry, where individual isotopes are resolved.

      How do I turn a molar mass into grams?

      Multiply moles by molar mass: grams = moles × g/mol. Two moles of glucose weigh 2 × 180.156 = 360.312 g. The Grams to Moles calculator does this step in both directions.

      What mass do I use for a hydrated salt?

      The form on the bottle. Anhydrous Na2CO3 is 105.988 g/mol; the decahydrate Na2CO3·10H2O is 286.138 g/mol. Weighing the decahydrate as if it were anhydrous under-doses by nearly two-thirds. Always read the dot and the water count before weighing.

      Can I find molar mass without a formula?

      For a gas, yes: weigh a known volume at a known pressure and temperature and use M = mRT ÷ PV, as worked in the gas-experiment section above. The result identifies a candidate, not a certainty — confirm it with a second measurement. For solids and liquids, some other measurement (freezing-point depression, titration, mass spectrometry) has to supply the missing information.

      Do ions have a different molar mass?

      Technically yes, by one electron mass (0.00055 g/mol) per unit of charge: (H3NSi)+ is 45.115 g/mol against 45.116 neutral. The difference sits below the last digit most tables print, so ordinary weighing and stoichiometry ignore it.

      Can I use this for a mixture or a polymer?

      Only as an average. A mixture takes the mole-fraction weighted average of its components; a polymer takes its repeating unit times the degree of polymerization, and real samples quote a number-average or weight-average because chains differ in length. Neither has one exact molar mass.

      How many decimals should I report?

      As many as your least precise measurement justifies. Most everyday calculations need only three or four significant figures; a balance reading to ±0.01 g does not justify five decimals. Keep full precision through the working and round once, at the end.
