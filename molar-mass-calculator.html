Molar Mass Calculator

      How the number is worked out

      Molar mass is the mass of one mole of a formula unit, in grams per mole. The arithmetic never changes: expand, count, multiply, add. Round once, at the end.

      1. Expand the formula. A subscript after a closing bracket multiplies everything inside it. A hydrate dot starts a second part, multiplied by its leading number.
      2. Count the atoms of each element.
      3. Multiply each count by that element’s standard atomic weight.
      4. Add the subtotals.

      Worked by hand: glucose, C6H12O6

      ElementAtomsAtomic weightSubtotal
      C612.01172.066
      H121.00812.096
      O615.99995.994
      Total24180.156

      So glucose is 180.156 g/mol = 0.180156 kg/mol = 180.156 Da. The same routine gives sulfuric acid, H2SO4: (2 × 1.008) + 32.06 + (4 × 15.999) = 98.072 g/mol.

      Traps worth knowing

      1. Co is not CO. Cobalt is 58.933 g/mol; carbon monoxide is 28.010. A capital starts a new element.
      2. A bracket subscript multiplies everything inside. Al2(SO4)3 holds 2 Al, 3 S and 12 O.
      3. The hydrate dot is not a decimal point. Leaving the waters out of CuSO4·5H2O loses 90.075 g/mol.
      4. Average is not monoisotopic. Chlorine’s 35.45 g/mol averages about 76% Cl-35 with 24% Cl-37. Mass spectrometry wants the monoisotopic figure instead.
      5. Round once. Keep full precision until the final line; rounding each subtotal first drifts the total.
      6. Some elements have no standard atomic weight. Many radioactive and synthetic ones do not; this calculator says so rather than inventing a value.
      Why another calculator may disagree by 0.01 to 0.07 g/mol. Almost always the atomic-weight table or the rounding rule. Water is 18.015 g/mol here and 18.016 with O = 16.00. A gap that small is an edition effect, not chemistry.

      Questions people actually ask

      Is molar mass the same as molecular weight?

      The numbers are identical for the same formula and the same atomic weights. Molar mass carries the unit g/mol; molecular weight is strictly a dimensionless ratio, and formula weight is the term for ionic salts such as NaCl.

      When do I use kg/mol?

      When the quantity scales up: divide g/mol by 1,000. Glucose is 180.156 g/mol = 0.180156 kg/mol. One kilomole of glucose weighs 180.156 kg.

      How do I enter a hydrate?

      Type the full formula with the dot, or an asterisk or full stop. CuSO4·5H2O is 249.677 g/mol, not the anhydrous 159.602.

      Do parentheses and brackets change the answer?

      Yes. A subscript after a closing bracket multiplies every atom inside it. K4[Fe(CN)6] holds 6 C and 6 N, and weighs 368.345 g/mol.

      Why does my answer differ slightly from another source?

      The atomic-weight table or the rounding rule, nearly every time. Check which values the other source uses before assuming either is wrong.
