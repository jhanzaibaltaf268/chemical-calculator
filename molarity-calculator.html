    The working

      The unit conversion is the whole trap: millilitres must become litres before they meet the molarity.

      Mass to weigh at a glance

      Two directions, one definition

      M = n ÷ V with V in litres, and grams = M × V(L) × molar mass. Purity divides at the end: a solid that is only 95% the compound needs theoretical mass ÷ 0.95 of powder. If you start from a weighed mass rather than moles, the Grams to Moles calculator gives the mole count first.

      Worked by hand: 250 mL of 0.500 M NaOH

      NaOH weighs 22.990 + 15.999 + 1.008 = 39.997 g/mol.

      1. Litres. 250 mL = 0.250 L.
      2. Moles. 0.500 × 0.250 = 0.125 mol.
      3. Mass. 0.125 × 39.997 = 5.00 g. Dissolve and make up to 250 mL — not add 250 mL of water, which would overshoot the volume.
      4. With 95% purity. 5.00 ÷ 0.95 = 5.26 g of powder.

      The default above runs the first direction: 1 mol in 1 L = 1 M exactly.

      Worked by hand: molarity from a weighed mass

      NaCl weighs 22.990 + 35.45 = 58.44 g/mol. Suppose 11.69 g is dissolved and made up to 500 mL.

      1. Moles. 11.69 ÷ 58.44 = 0.200 mol (to three figures).
      2. Litres. 500 mL = 0.500 L.
      3. Molarity. 0.200 ÷ 0.500 = 0.400 M.

      Grams alone never give molarity: divide by the molar mass first, then by litres.

      Worked by hand: 100 mL of 250 mM glucose

      Glucose (C6H12O6) weighs 6 × 12.011 + 12 × 1.008 + 6 × 15.999 = 180.156 g/mol.

      1. Millimolar to molar. 250 mM ÷ 1,000 = 0.250 M.
      2. Litres. 100 mL = 0.100 L.
      3. Moles. 0.250 × 0.100 = 0.0250 mol.
      4. Mass. 0.0250 × 180.156 = 4.50 g. Dissolve and make up to 100 mL.

      Make up to the mark: the procedure

      The final-volume rule is a procedure, not just a warning. Weigh the solid, dissolve it in part of the solvent in a beaker, and pour that solution into a volumetric flask. Rinse the beaker and the stirring rod into the flask — the rinse carries the last of the solute with it, and skipping it leaves real moles behind. Then add solvent until the bottom of the meniscus sits on the flask’s line, and mix thoroughly. Only now is the volume the volume in the calculation. Adding the full measured volume of solvent to the solid instead gives a larger final volume, because the solid itself takes up space, and a lower molarity than planned.

      The liquid rises to the dashed mark: dissolve first, fill to the line last.

      Diluting a stock: M₁V₁ = M₂V₂

      Dilution keeps the moles and changes the volume: M₁V₁ = M₂V₂, with both volumes in the same unit. Worked: make 500 mL of 0.100 M from a 1.000 M stock. V₁ = (0.100 × 0.500) ÷ 1.000 = 0.050 L, so measure 50 mL of stock and make up to 500 mL. For very low concentrations, split the dilution into steps instead of pipetting a tiny volume once: 1 M to 0.01 M goes as 10 mL of 1 M made up to 100 mL (0.1 M), then 10 mL of that made up to 100 mL again (0.01 M). Each step uses an easy volume, so the pipetting error stays small. For multi-step or repeated dilutions, the Dilution calculator carries the chain.

      Same moles, more water

      Topping up a finished solution dilutes it: the moles stay, the volume grows, the molarity falls in proportion. The two columns hold the same 0.125 mol, at different volumes.

      Molarity, molality, and the other concentration units

      Molarity is not the only way to say how strong a solution is, and the conversions run through the molar mass. Molality divides moles by kilograms of solvent instead of litres of solution, so it does not move with temperature. Mass-per-volume percent converts as w/v% = (M × MW) ÷ 10: a 0.500 M glucose solution is (0.500 × 180.156) ÷ 10 = 9.01% w/v, and backwards, 9.01% w/v glucose is 9.01 × 10 ÷ 180.156 = 0.500 M. Mass-per-mass percent needs the solution’s density as well. Parts per million suits very dilute solutions: 0.001 M NaCl is 0.001 × 58.44 × 1,000 = 58.44 ppm. Normality counts reactive equivalents instead of moles: 1 M sulfuric acid is 2 N, because each formula unit supplies two H⁺, while 1 M NaOH is 1 N. Protein solutions are usually quoted in mg/mL and µM instead — the Protein Molarity calculator converts between them.

      Temperature moves molarity

      Molarity is defined at a temperature, because volume changes with it. Warm a solution and it expands, so the same moles occupy more litres and the molarity drops. Near room temperature water expands roughly 0.021% per degree, so a 1.000 M solution made at 20 °C reads about 1.000 ÷ 1.0021 = 0.998 M at 30 °C — small, but larger than most weighing errors, and the reason precise work states the temperature. Molality, dividing by kilograms of solvent, has no such drift, which is why it is preferred when temperature varies.

      1. What mass of KCl do you weigh for 250 mL of 0.200 M?
      2. 5.00 g of NaOH is made up to 500 mL. What is the molarity?
      3. You dilute 25 mL of 2.00 M stock to 250 mL. What is the new molarity?

      Answers: (1) 0.200 × 0.250 × 74.548 = 3.73 g. (2) 5.00 ÷ 39.997 = 0.1250 mol, 0.1250 ÷ 0.500 = 0.250 M. (3) (2.00 × 0.025) ÷ 0.250 = 0.200 M.

      Traps worth knowing

      1. Millilitres are not litres. 250 mL of 0.500 M NaOH needs 0.125 mol, not 125 mol. Divide mL by 1,000 first.
      2. Make up to, not add to. Adding 250 mL of water to the solid gives more than 250 mL of solution, and a weaker molarity than planned.
      3. Which form? Weigh the hydrate with the hydrate’s molar mass, the anhydrous salt with its own.
      4. Purity counted once. Theoretical mass ÷ purity fraction. Multiplying by purity instead understates the weigh-out.
      5. M is not mM. 0.5 M is 500 mM. A factor of 1,000 hides in the prefix, not in the arithmetic.
      6. Moles are not grams. 1 mol of NaCl weighs 58.44 g, not 1 g. Divide grams by the molar mass before dividing by litres.
      7. mg/mL is not molarity. 1 mg/mL is 1 g/L, so for NaCl it is 1 ÷ 58.44 = 0.0171 M (17.1 mM). Divide by the molar mass to cross from mass concentration to molarity.
      8. Topping up dilutes a finished solution. Topping 250 mL of 0.500 M (0.125 mol) up to 500 mL gives 0.125 ÷ 0.500 = 0.250 M. The moles stay; the molarity halves.
      9. Normality is not molarity. 1 M H2SO4 is 2 N, because each unit supplies two H⁺. Using the molarity where normality is asked for halves the answer for a diprotic acid.
      10. Warm solutions read weaker. A solution made at 20 °C and read at 30 °C loses about 0.2% of its molarity to expansion. State the temperature for precise work, or use molality.
      The volume is the expensive one. Everything else in this calculation is a single multiplication, so a mL–litre slip is a 1,000× error that no amount of careful weighing fixes. Convert the volume first, every time.

      Questions people actually ask

      How do I calculate molarity?

      Divide moles by litres. 1 mol in 1 L is 1 M; 0.125 mol in 0.250 L is 0.500 M.

      How much do I weigh for a target molarity?

      Grams = molarity × litres × molar mass. For 250 mL of 0.500 M NaOH: 0.500 × 0.250 × 39.997 = 5.00 g.

      Do I add the final volume of water?

      No. Dissolve the solid in part of the water, transfer it, then top up to the final volume. Adding the full volume gives a larger volume and a lower molarity.

      How does purity change the weigh-out?

      Divide the theoretical mass by the purity fraction. At 95% purity, 5.00 g theoretical needs 5.26 g of powder.

      What is the difference between M and mM?

      A factor of 1,000. 1 M = 1,000 mM. Check the prefix before the number, especially on protocols copied from another lab.

      How do I convert mM to M?

      Divide by 1,000. 250 mM = 0.250 M; going the other way, multiply by 1,000, so 0.500 M = 500 mM.

      How do I find molarity from a weighed mass?

      Divide grams by the molar mass to get moles, then divide by litres. For 11.69 g of NaCl (58.44 g/mol) made up to 0.500 L: 11.69 ÷ 58.44 = 0.200 mol, and 0.200 ÷ 0.500 = 0.400 M.

      How much NaCl do I weigh for 100 mL of 1 M?

      5.84 g: 1.000 × 0.100 × 58.44 = 5.844 g, rounded to three figures. Dissolve it and make up to 100 mL.

      What if my solid is a hydrate?

      Use the hydrate’s molar mass, not the anhydrous salt’s. Copper(II) sulfate pentahydrate (CuSO4·5H2O) weighs 249.68 g/mol, against 159.60 g/mol for anhydrous CuSO4, so weighing with the wrong one throws the molarity out by more than a third.

      How do I dilute a stock solution?

      Use M₁V₁ = M₂V₂ with both volumes in the same unit. For 500 mL of 0.100 M from 1.000 M stock: (0.100 × 0.500) ÷ 1.000 = 0.050 L, so 50 mL of stock made up to 500 mL. The Dilution calculator handles multi-step dilutions.

      What is the difference between molarity and molality?

      Molarity divides moles by litres of solution; molality divides moles by kilograms of solvent. Molarity shifts with temperature because volume does; molality does not.

      What is normality?

      Equivalents per litre: N = M × equivalents per formula unit. 1 M H2SO4 is 2 N (two H⁺ per unit); 1 M NaOH is 1 N. Normality belongs to titrations; molarity belongs everywhere else.

      Why did my molarity change when the lab warmed up?

      Expansion. The same moles in a larger volume give a lower molarity — about 0.2% lower for a 10 °C rise near room temperature. The moles did not change; the litres did.

      How do I convert w/v% to molarity?

      M = w/v% × 10 ÷ molar mass. 9.01% w/v glucose is 9.01 × 10 ÷ 180.156 = 0.500 M. Mass-per-mass percent needs the density as well.
