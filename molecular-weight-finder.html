    Why the name is the hard part

    Most people know a compound by a name, not a formula. Names are looser. One name can point to several forms: copper sulfate is the anhydrous salt CuSO4 in one bottle and the pentahydrate CuSO4·5H2O in another. One formula can point to several compounds: glucose and fructose are both C6H12O6. So two questions have to be settled before any arithmetic: which compound, and which form.

    Copper sulfate has two correct answers

    • Anhydrous, CuSO4: 63.546 + 32.06 + 4 × 15.999 = 159.602 g/mol.
    • Pentahydrate, CuSO4·5H2O: 159.602 + 5 × 18.015 = 249.677 g/mol. The five waters are 90.075 g/mol, about 36% of the total.

    Weigh a pentahydrate using the anhydrous weight and you hold 159.602 ÷ 249.677 = 0.639 of the moles you think you have. Check the bottle before you calculate.

    Aspirin, resolved by name

    Aspirin is the trade name for acetylsalicylic acid, C9H8O4. Both names mean the same compound in the same form, so there is one answer: 9 × 12.011 + 8 × 1.008 + 4 × 15.999 = 180.159 g/mol. A 500 mg tablet of pure aspirin is 0.500 ÷ 180.159 = 2.78 mmol.

    Washing soda, the second ambiguous name

    Copper sulfate is not the only name with two answers. Washing soda returns both the anhydrous salt and the decahydrate: Na2CO3 at 2 × 22.990 + 12.011 + 3 × 15.999 = 105.988 g/mol, and Na2CO3·10H2O at 105.988 + 10 × 18.015 = 286.138 g/mol — where the ten waters are 180.150 g/mol, about 63% of the total. The decahydrate weighs nearly three times the anhydrous salt under the same name. The label, again, decides.

    One formula, many names — and one name, no compound

    Names fan out in both directions. Aspirin, acetylsalicylic acid and ASA are one compound at 180.159 g/mol; baking soda, sodium bicarbonate and sodium hydrogen carbonate are one compound at 84.006 g/mol. Sucrose and lactose are the second isomer pair after glucose and fructose: both C12H22O11, both 12 × 12.011 + 22 × 1.008 + 11 × 15.999 = 342.297 g/mol. And some familiar words are not compounds at all: typing “sugar” alone returns near matches, not a weight, because sugar is a class — blood sugar, fruit sugar, table sugar and milk sugar are four different compounds. A name lookup that silently picked one of them would be guessing.

    The name-to-weight chain, end to end

    Every lookup on this page runs the same four steps. Worked for epsom salt: (1) the name resolves to magnesium sulfate heptahydrate, MgSO4·7H2O — the name alone fixes the form here, unlike copper sulfate; (2) the formula splits into MgSO4 plus seven waters; (3) MgSO4 is 24.305 + 32.06 + 4 × 15.999 = 120.361 g/mol, and the waters are 7 × 18.015 = 126.105 g/mol; (4) the total is 246.466 g/mol. If you already know the formula and want to skip the lookup, the Molar Mass calculator takes it directly. Once the formula is settled, the Percent Composition calculator breaks it into mass shares.

    When the counterion is the trap

    A hydrochloride adds HCl, 36.458 g/mol, to the free base. The page’s “roughly 15%” is exact for a base near 207 g/mol: 36.458 ÷ (206.6 + 36.458) = 0.150. For a lighter base the share is bigger, for a heavier base smaller — so the trap scales with the drug, and the only safe move is the label: if it does not state the salt, the weight does not state the dose.

    Try it yourself

    1. Washing soda, decahydrate: Na2CO3 is 105.988 g/mol. What is Na2CO3·10H2O, and what share of it is water?
    2. Epsom salt is MgSO4·7H2O. MgSO4 is 120.361 g/mol. What is the heptahydrate?
    3. A free base weighs 206.6 g/mol. What does its hydrochloride weigh, and what share of the salt is HCl?

    Answers: (1) 105.988 + 180.150 = 286.138 g/mol; water is 180.150 ÷ 286.138 = 63.0%. (2) 120.361 + 126.105 = 246.466 g/mol. (3) 206.6 + 36.458 = 243.058 g/mol; HCl is 36.458 ÷ 243.058 = 15.0%.

    Five questions before you accept a match

    1. Common, systematic or trade name? Aspirin and acetylsalicylic acid are one compound. Check the systematic name when a trade name is ambiguous.
    2. Which form — hydrate, salt, acid or base? Copper sulfate, washing soda and drug hydrochlorides all change the weight.
    3. Could the same formula be a different compound? Glucose and fructose are both 180.156 g/mol. The name separates them; the formula cannot.
    4. Is it a mixture or a polymer? Gasoline, starch, steel and milk have no single molecular weight. “No single value” is the correct answer, not a number.
    5. Element, compound or ion? Iron is 55.845; iron(III) oxide is 159.687. Roman numerals are oxidation states, not atom counts.
    6. Trusting the first match for an ambiguous name. Copper sulfate, washing soda and magnesium sulfate each return more than one form, and the page shows the first while listing the rest. The first is not the right one by default — the label is.
    7. A class word typed as a compound. Sugar, protein, starch and gasoline are classes, mixtures or polymers, not compounds. The page answers with near matches or “no single value” rather than a number, and that refusal is the correct result.
    Forgetting the counterion. A hydrochloride adds HCl, 36.458 g/mol, to the free base. Weighing by the base weight understates the moles of salt you actually have — roughly 15% for many drugs.

    Questions people actually ask

    Can I search by name instead of formula?

    Yes. The match, its formula and its form are shown before the number, so you can confirm it is the compound in your hand.

    What if a compound has several names?

    Aliases of one compound give one weight. Aspirin and acetylsalicylic acid are both C9H8O4 = 180.159 g/mol; baking soda and sodium bicarbonate are both NaHCO3 = 84.006 g/mol.

    Why does copper sulfate return two weights?

    The name covers the anhydrous salt at 159.602 g/mol and the pentahydrate at 249.677 g/mol. Use the form printed on your label.

    What if two compounds share a formula?

    They weigh the same. Glucose and fructose are both 180.156 g/mol. The name tells them apart; the weight cannot.

    Can I look up a brand-name drug?

    Only when the name maps to an active ingredient and a salt form. If the result does not state the salt, such as a hydrochloride, do not use it for dosing.

    What happens when the name is not found?

    You get near matches or a request for the formula. Returning the wrong compound silently is worse than returning nothing.

    What is the difference between a trade name and a systematic name?

    Only the naming system. Aspirin is the trade name, acetylsalicylic acid the systematic name, and both resolve to C9H8O4 = 180.159 g/mol. Blue vitriol is the old trade-style name for copper(II) sulfate pentahydrate, 249.677 g/mol. The weight follows the compound, not the name’s style.

    Why do I get several matches for one name?

    Because the name covers several forms. Copper sulfate returns the anhydrous salt (159.602 g/mol) and the pentahydrate (249.677 g/mol); washing soda returns the anhydrous salt (105.988 g/mol) and the decahydrate (286.138 g/mol). Pick the form printed on your label.

    What happens when I type a mixture or a polymer?

    You get “no single molecular weight” and the reason: gasoline is a mixture of hydrocarbons, starch a polymer of variable chain length, steel an alloy. A single number for any of them would be invented, so the page refuses instead.

    Can I type a formula here instead of a name?

    Yes. When a name is not in the library, the page tries the input as a formula and calculates that. If you already know the formula, the Molar Mass calculator takes it directly without the lookup step.

    How do I check a drug’s salt form before weighing?

    Read the label for the salt: hydrochloride, sulfate, sodium, or nothing stated. A hydrochloride adds 36.458 g/mol to the free base — about 15% for a base near 207 g/mol. If the label does not state a salt, do not use the free-base weight for dosing.
