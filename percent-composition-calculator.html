    What percent composition means

    Each element’s share of a compound’s total mass: % element = (mass of that element in one mole ÷ molar mass) × 100. It always means mass unless something says otherwise.

    Worked by hand: ammonium sulfate, (NH4)2SO4

    The bracket is the step most often skipped. (NH4)2 holds 2 N and 8 H — not 4 H. With the sulfate group the formula is 15 atoms: N 2, H 8, S 1, O 4.

    Hydrates count their water

    In CuSO4·5H2O the dot is a separator, not a decimal point, and the 5 multiplies the whole water unit: 9 O and 10 H in total. The compound is 249.677 g/mol, of which water is 90.075 — 36.08%. Heating 100 g drives off about 36.08 g of water and leaves about 63.92 g of anhydrous salt.

    The hydrate, element by element

    The water share is only one line of the pentahydrate’s story. CuSO4·5H2O at 249.677 g/mol breaks down as Cu 63.546 ÷ 249.677 = 25.45%, S 32.06 ÷ 249.677 = 12.84%, O 9 × 15.999 = 143.991 ÷ 249.677 = 57.67%, H 10 × 1.008 = 10.080 ÷ 249.677 = 4.04% — and they sum to 100.00%, the built-in check.     Ten hydrogens, four percent of the mass: atom count is not mass share.

    Water, two ways

    Water is the sharpest mass-versus-atom lesson there is. By atoms, H2O is 66.67% hydrogen and 33.33% oxygen. By mass, it is 11.19% hydrogen and 88.81% oxygen — because one oxygen atom outweighs two hydrogens nearly eight times over. Bars are hydrogen’s share, both ways.

    Hydrogen, by mass — 11.19%

    Hydrogen, by atoms — 66.67%

    Same molecule, same element, two honest numbers answering two different questions.

    Why two sources print 53.27% and 53.28%

    Glucose oxygen is 95.994 ÷ 180.156 = 53.28% on this page’s weights (C 40.00%, H 6.71%, O 53.28%). Another source printing 53.27% is not wrong and not a counting error — it uses a slightly different atomic-weight table, and the second decimal moves with the table. Compare percentages only on the same weight set, the way the traps warn about mixing tables partway through.

    From percent back to grams

    Percent composition runs in both directions. A 10 g sample of CuSO4·5H2O holds 10 × 25.45% = 2.545 g of copper — the number that matters when the question is what you actually weigh out. Set the sample mass to 100 g and the grams column reads as the percent column, exactly. The Element Mass calculator runs this direction for any sample and any element.

    The same percentages, several formulas

    Formaldehyde, CH2O, is 12.011 + 2.016 + 15.999 = 30.026 g/mol, and its percentages are C 40.00%, H 6.71%, O 53.28% — identical to glucose’s, because glucose is six CH2O units: 180.156 ÷ 30.026 = 6.0 exactly. Percent composition gives the empirical formula, never the molecular one; the molar mass is what separates CH2O from C6H12O6. The Empirical Formula calculator runs the backwards direction from percentages to the simplest ratio.

    Try it yourself

    1. CuSO4·5H2O is 249.677 g/mol. What is copper’s mass percent, and how many grams of copper are in 10 g?
    2. Water is 18.015 g/mol. What is hydrogen’s mass percent, and what is its atom percent?
    3. CH2O is 30.026 g/mol and glucose is 180.156 g/mol. Why do they share percentages, and what separates them?

    Answers: (1) 63.546 ÷ 249.677 = 25.45%; 10 × 25.45% = 2.545 g. (2) Mass: 2.016 ÷ 18.015 = 11.19%; atoms: 2 ÷ 3 = 66.67%. (3) Glucose is six CH2O units (180.156 ÷ 30.026 = 6.0), so the ratios match; the molar mass separates them.

    Traps worth knowing

    • Forgetting the bracket multiplier. (NH4)2SO4 expands to N2H8SO4.
    • Dropping the water of hydration. Using 159.602 for CuSO4·5H2O understates the molar mass by 36% and changes every percentage.
    • Reading atom percent as mass percent. See the table above.
    • Mixing atomic-weight tables partway through. O = 16.00 for one element and 15.999 for another breaks the 100% check.
    • Panicking over rounding. A rounded sum of 99.99% or 100.01% is normal. More than about 0.1% off means an atom count or an atomic weight is wrong.
    • Confusing it with the empirical formula. The same percentages fit several molecular formulas; molar mass is what separates them.
    • Quoting the anhydrous percentage for the hydrate. Copper is 39.82% of anhydrous CuSO4 (63.546 ÷ 159.602) but only 25.45% of the pentahydrate. The percentage belongs to the form, not just the element.
    • Dividing by the sample mass instead of the molar mass. Nitrogen in (NH4)2SO4 is 28.014 ÷ 132.134 = 21.20%, not 28.014 ÷ 250 = 11.21%. Percent composition is per mole, never per sample.

    Questions people actually ask

    Should the percentages add up to 100%?

    Yes, within rounding. 99.99% or 100.01% from rounded values is fine; more than about 0.1% off means something is wrong.

    What is the difference between mass percent and atom percent?

    Mass percent is the share of total mass; atom percent is the share of atoms. In (NH4)2SO4 hydrogen is 53.33% of the atoms and 6.10% of the mass.

    How do I enter a hydrate?

    Type the full formula with the dot, an asterisk or a full stop. All the water’s atoms are counted: CuSO4·5H2O has 9 O and 10 H, and water is 36.08% of its mass.

    How do I go backwards to a formula?

    Assume 100 g so each percentage becomes grams, then divide by atomic weights and reduce the ratio. The empirical formula calculator does exactly that.

    Can I use it for mixtures or alloys?

    Not directly. This works on a pure compound’s formula unit. For a mixture, work out each component separately.

    Why does glucose show 53.27% oxygen on another site?

    Different atomic-weight tables move the second decimal. On this page’s weights, oxygen is 95.994 ÷ 180.156 = 53.28%. A source printing 53.27% is using slightly different weights, not making a counting error.

    How many grams of copper are in 10 g of copper sulfate pentahydrate?

    2.545 g. Copper is 63.546 ÷ 249.677 = 25.45% of CuSO4·5H2O, so 10 × 25.45% = 2.545 g. The Element Mass calculator does this for any sample.

    Can two different compounds have the same percent composition?

    Yes. Formaldehyde (CH2O, 30.026 g/mol) and glucose (C6H12O6, 180.156 g/mol) are both C 40.00%, H 6.71%, O 53.28%, because glucose is six CH2O units. Only the molar mass separates them.

    What is atom percent used for?

    Questions that count atoms rather than weigh mass: out of every 100 atoms in water, 66.67 are hydrogen. Mass percent answers weighing questions; atom percent answers counting questions, and the two must never be swapped.

    How do I enter a nested formula like Ca3(PO4)2?

    Type it as written: the bracket multiplier applies to everything inside. Ca3(PO4)2 is Ca 3, P 2, O 8, at 310.174 g/mol — Ca 38.76%, P 19.97%, O 41.26%.
