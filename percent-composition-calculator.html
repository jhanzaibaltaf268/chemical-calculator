    What percent composition means

    Each element’s share of a compound’s total mass: % element = (mass of that element in one mole ÷ molar mass) × 100. It always means mass unless something says otherwise.

    Worked by hand: ammonium sulfate, (NH4)2SO4

    The bracket is the step most often skipped. (NH4)2 holds 2 N and 8 H — not 4 H. With the sulfate group the formula is 15 atoms: N 2, H 8, S 1, O 4.

    Hydrates count their water

    In CuSO4·5H2O the dot is a separator, not a decimal point, and the 5 multiplies the whole water unit: 9 O and 10 H in total. The compound is 249.677 g/mol, of which water is 90.075 — 36.08%. Heating 100 g drives off about 36.08 g of water and leaves about 63.92 g of anhydrous salt.

    Traps worth knowing

    • Forgetting the bracket multiplier. (NH4)2SO4 expands to N2H8SO4.
    • Dropping the water of hydration. Using 159.602 for CuSO4·5H2O understates the molar mass by 36% and changes every percentage.
    • Reading atom percent as mass percent. See the table above.
    • Mixing atomic-weight tables partway through. O = 16.00 for one element and 15.999 for another breaks the 100% check.
    • Panicking over rounding. A rounded sum of 99.99% or 100.01% is normal. More than about 0.1% off means an atom count or an atomic weight is wrong.
    • Confusing it with the empirical formula. The same percentages fit several molecular formulas; molar mass is what separates them.

    Questions people actually ask

    Should the percentages add up to 100%?

    Yes, within rounding. 99.99% or 100.01% from rounded values is fine; more than about 0.1% off means something is wrong.

    What is the difference between mass percent and atom percent?

    Mass percent is the share of total mass; atom percent is the share of atoms. In (NH4)2SO4 hydrogen is 53.33% of the atoms and 6.10% of the mass.

    How do I enter a hydrate?

    Type the full formula with the dot, an asterisk or a full stop. All the water’s atoms are counted: CuSO4·5H2O has 9 O and 10 H, and water is 36.08% of its mass.

    How do I go backwards to a formula?

    Assume 100 g so each percentage becomes grams, then divide by atomic weights and reduce the ratio. The empirical formula calculator does exactly that.

    Can I use it for mixtures or alloys?

    Not directly. This works on a pure compound’s formula unit. For a mixture, work out each component separately.
