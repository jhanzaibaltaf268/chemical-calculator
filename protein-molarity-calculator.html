      Working dilution

      The two conversions

      µM = (µg/mL) ÷ (MW in kDa) and mg/mL = µM × MW(kDa) ÷ 1000. Check against a known case: 2 mg/mL of BSA at 66.5 kDa is 2000 ÷ 66.5 = 30.1 µM.

      Worked by hand: a lyophilised vial to a 10 µM working solution

      A vial holds 10 mg of powder at 90% protein by certificate, and the protein is 45 kDa.

      1. Correct for purity. 10 mg × 0.90 = 9.0 mg of actual protein. Skipping this makes everything downstream about 11% too high.
      2. Make the stock. Dissolve and make up to 2.0 mL — not add 2.0 mL. That is 9.0 ÷ 2.0 = 4.5 mg/mL.
      3. Convert. 4500 µg/mL ÷ 45 kDa = 100 µM. The long way agrees: 4.5 g/L ÷ 45,000 g/mol = 1.0 × 10−4 mol/L.
      4. Dilute. For 500 µL at 10 µM: take (10 × 500) ÷ 100 = 50 µL of stock and 450 µL of buffer. Adding 500 µL of buffer instead gives 550 µL at 9.09 µM.

      What 1 mg/mL comes to

      For any protein, 1 mg/mL = 1,000,000 ÷ MW in µM.

      Same mg/mL, different molecule counts

      1 mg/mL of lysozyme (14,310 Da) is 69.88 µM. 1 mg/mL of IgG (150,000 Da) is 6.67 µM. Same mass concentration, but the lysozyme solution holds 69.88 ÷ 6.67 = 10.48 times as many molecules per mL — 4.21 × 1016 against 4.01 × 1015. That gap is the whole reason for the conversion: comparing two proteins by mg/mL compares their masses, not their molecule counts, and activity, binding and stoichiometry all run on molecules.

      Identical mass concentrations, tenfold different molarities.

      Where the mg/mL figure comes from

      The mg/mL figure itself often comes from absorbance: A280 with an extinction coefficient gives mass concentration, and the conversion to µM still needs the molecular weight after that. The weight itself comes from the sequence — UniProtKB lists it, or the Protein Molecular Weight calculator computes it from the one-letter sequence — but the sequence weight is the starting point, not the final answer: tags, glycosylation and the mature-vs-precursor question (the traps above) still change it.

      The wrong weight moves the answer directly

      µM scales inversely with the weight entered, so a weight error becomes a molarity error of the same size, inverted. A 45 kDa protein entered as its 47 kDa precursor (signal peptide still counted) gives a µM figure 4.26% low. A GFP fusion counted without its ~27 kDa tag — 45 kDa entered for a 72 kDa construct — gives a µM figure 37.5% low. The BSA range (66,430–66,500 Da) moves the answer by only 0.11%, which is why small source differences are harmless and construct differences are not.

      Purity, once more, at 80%

      A vial labelled 1 mg that is 80% protein holds 0.8 mg. At 45 kDa in 1 mL, the uncorrected figure is 1,000 ÷ 45 = 22.22 µM; the corrected figure is 800 ÷ 45 = 17.78 µM, 20% lower. Purity scales both mg/mL and µM by the same factor — it is not a correction that applies to one and not the other. And the step from that stock to a working solution is the Dilution calculator’s C₁V₁ = C₂V₂, as the working-dilution panel above already shows.

      Try it yourself

      1. 1 mg/mL of lysozyme (14,310 Da) and 1 mg/mL of IgG (150,000 Da): what is each in µM, and how many times more molecules does the lysozyme solution hold?
      2. A vial labelled 1 mg at 80% purity, 45 kDa, made up to 1 mL: what is the corrected µM?
      3. A 45 kDa construct entered as 72 kDa with its GFP fusion counted: by what percentage is the µM figure off?

      Answers: (1) 69.88 µM and 6.67 µM; 10.48 times more molecules. (2) 800 ÷ 45 = 17.78 µM (uncorrected: 22.22 µM). (3) 45 ÷ 72 = 0.625, so 37.5% low — and entering the precursor at 47 kDa instead gives 4.26% low.

      Before you trust the molecular weight

      1. Which weight? The certificate value for the lot in hand, or the sequence weight of your exact construct.
      2. Mature or precursor? Check whether the signal peptide is removed.
      3. Tags counted? A His-tag is small; a GFP fusion adds about 27 kDa.
      4. Glycosylated? Sequence predictions understate glycoproteins. Use the measured value when the assay counts whole molecules.
      5. Monomer or oligomer? State the basis. Dimer µM is monomer µM divided by two.
      6. Salt or hydrate form? Divide by the salt molar mass, not the free base — typically a 10% difference.
      7. Purity applied once? Follow the certificate’s own definition and do not count it twice.
      8. Comparing two proteins by mg/mL. 1 mg/mL of lysozyme is 69.88 µM; 1 mg/mL of IgG is 6.67 µM. Same mass, tenfold different molecule counts — compare in µM whenever molecules matter.
      9. Average against monoisotopic weight. The µM figure inherits whichever basis you enter. For a 45 kDa protein the gap is small, but it is still a basis to state, not a detail to ignore — especially when the assay counts whole molecules.
      Da against kDa is the expensive one. Entering 66.5 where daltons are expected gives a molarity 1,000× too high. No amount of careful rounding fixes a unit error — check the unit first. Likewise, 1 µg/µL is 1 mg/mL, not a thousandth of it, and µM means µmol/L, never µmol/mL.

      Questions people actually ask

      How do I convert mg/mL to µM?

      Divide µg/mL by the molecular weight in kDa. For 2 mg/mL BSA at 66.5 kDa: 2000 ÷ 66.5 = 30.1 µM.

      How does purity change the result?

      Multiply the weighed mass by the purity fraction first. 10 mg at 90% is 9.0 mg of protein; uncorrected results run about 11% high.

      Monomer or dimer molecular weight?

      State the basis. The usual convention is monomer weight and monomer molarity; dimer molarity is that divided by the number of subunits.

      How much protein do I weigh for a target molarity?

      Mass in grams = molarity (mol/L) × volume (L) × MW (g/mol). For 10 mL of 20 µM at 50 kDa, 10 mg of actual protein.

      Why does my A280 disagree with my Bradford?

      They measure different things. A280 depends on the extinction coefficient and is inflated by nucleic acids and scattering; Bradford and BCA responses vary between proteins and react to detergents and reductants. Check the molecular weight basis, the blank and the dilution factor before blaming either method.

      Why is 1 mg/mL of lysozyme not 1 mg/mL of IgG in µM?

      Because µM divides by molecular weight. Lysozyme at 14,310 Da gives 69.88 µM; IgG at 150,000 Da gives 6.67 µM — 10.48 times fewer molecules at the same mass concentration.

      Where do I get the molecular weight?

      From the sequence: UniProtKB lists it, or the Protein Molecular Weight calculator computes it from the one-letter sequence. Then check tags, glycosylation and the mature form against your actual construct.

      How much does a wrong weight cost?

      µM scales inversely with the weight entered. A 45 kDa construct entered as 72 kDa with its GFP fusion gives a µM figure 37.5% low; a precursor entered at 47 kDa gives 4.26% low; the BSA source range (66,430–66,500 Da) costs only 0.11%.

      Does purity change µM or just mg/mL?

      Both, by the same factor. A vial labelled 1 mg at 80% purity holds 0.8 mg, so at 45 kDa in 1 mL the corrected figure is 17.78 µM, not the uncorrected 22.22 µM.
